# Portfolio risk: covariance, nonlinear losses, and evidence beyond VaR

A portfolio can have nearly zero delta and still lose when prices move. A curve model can explain 99.5% of total factor variance and miss all the risk of a particular spread. A reported 95% value at risk can be zero while the average loss in the worst 5% is substantial. These are different failure mechanisms: linearization, dimension reduction, and compression of a loss distribution into one quantile.

The closing lecture supplies the practical spine: portfolio covariance, duration-based bond VaR, cash-flow mapping, principal components, derivative deltas and gammas, and a currency forward represented by two discounted currency payments. We develop each with its units and assumptions visible, then add nonlinear revaluation, atom-aware expected shortfall, backtesting, and stress tests. The aim is a risk calculation whose answer can be traced to a stated position, horizon, factor model, and loss convention.

## 1. Start with a signed monetary loss [00:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xuUX0FVxwv0&t=26s)

Fix today's positions and a horizon $h$. Let $V_0$ be their initial value and $V_h$ their horizon value including the cash flows specified by the experiment. Define loss $L=V_0-V_h$: a positive number means a loss. The physical scenario distribution describes future outcomes; a pricing formula used inside each scenario can use replication or pricing probabilities without changing those physical scenario weights.

Let $X$ be a vector of factor changes with mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$. For a local revaluation with fixed sensitivities $a=\nabla V$, the approximation is $\Delta V\approx a^\mathsf{T}X$, hence

$$\mu_L=-a^\mathsf{T}\mu,\qquad
\sigma_L^2=a^\mathsf{T}\Sigma a.$$

The covariance formula needs finite second moments; it does not require a Gaussian distribution. Gaussianity is an additional assumption used to turn those moments into a quantile. If $X$ is jointly Gaussian and the linear model is exact, $L$ is Gaussian and

$$\operatorname{VaR}_{\alpha}(L)=\mu_L+z_{\alpha}\sigma_L,
\qquad z_{\alpha}=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha).$$

Here VaR is the left quantile $\inf\{l:\Pr(L\le l)\ge\alpha\}$, with no automatic floor at zero. Deterministic loss is handled directly if variance vanishes. A mean-zero approximation is a modeling choice justified, if at all, by the horizon and the intended use; it is not a consequence of Black–Scholes pricing. Omitting carry, theta, distributions, or funding changes what $L$ measures and must be stated.

For stock simple returns $X_i=\Delta S_i/S_i$, an option portfolio's exposure is $a_i=\Delta_iS_i$, where $\Delta_i$ includes signed quantities and contract multipliers. Its units are currency per unit return. If the factors are decimal yield changes, duration exposures have units currency per unit yield instead. Multiplying a basis-point covariance by a decimal-yield exposure without conversion creates a factor-of-$10^8$ error in variance.

The source's historical Microsoft/AT&T teaching case has deltas 1,000 and 20,000, stock prices 120 and 30, daily return standard deviations 2% and 1%, and correlation 0.3. The monetary one-standard-deviation contributions are 2,400 and 6,000. Consequently

$$\sigma_L^2=2400^2+6000^2+2(0.3)(2400)(6000)
=50{,}400{,}000,$$

giving $\sigma_L=7099.295740$. The labels and prices are source examples, not current market estimates. Negative positions must retain their signs inside the covariance sum. A positive semidefinite covariance is also essential: a negative computed variance is evidence of inconsistent inputs or implementation, not negative risk.

### Horizon scaling needs a time-series assumption

For $n$ independent identically distributed daily linear gains, covariance becomes $n\Sigma$ and mean becomes $n\mu$. The standard deviation scales by $\sqrt n$, while the mean scales by $n$. With serial dependence, variance instead includes the lagged covariance terms. A nonlinear portfolio, changing exposures, stochastic volatility, jumps, or active rebalancing can invalidate scaling one day's VaR by $\sqrt n$. Even an exact sum of Gaussian factor increments need not give a Gaussian nonlinear portfolio loss.

## 2. Duration VaR controls a parallel direction [05:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xuUX0FVxwv0&t=305s)

Under continuous-compounding yield $y$, a fixed positive cash-flow bond has price $B=\sum_k C_ke^{-yt_k}$ and duration $D=\sum_k t_kC_ke^{-yt_k}/B$. A small yield change gives $\Delta B\approx-BD\Delta y$. For a positive long portfolio with value $B_P$ and value-weighted duration $D_P$, a *common parallel* yield shock therefore produces

$$L\approx B_PD_P\Delta y,\qquad
\sigma_L=B_PD_P\sigma_{\Delta y}.$$

For signed portfolios the robust object is the sum of dollar sensitivities, not an average divided by a possibly zero net portfolio value. Chapter 9 explains the compounding conversion and DV01 scale. In particular, a zero-cost duration spread can have large offsetting gross sensitivities even though dividing by its net value is impossible.

The source gives value six million, duration 5.2 years, and daily decimal yield standard deviation 0.0009. Daily monetary standard deviation is 28,080. Under zero mean, independent Gaussian daily parallel shifts, its 20-day 90% VaR is

$$z_{0.90}\sqrt{20}(28080)=160934.141192.$$

Using the lecture's rounded $z=1.28$ instead gives 160739.299349. The difference comes from the quantile approximation; neither calculation verifies the distributional or parallel-shift assumptions. The initial curve need not be flat for a specified parallel shift of continuous zero rates. What matters is that the risk coordinate moves every relevant maturity by the same amount.

For maturity-specific curve shocks $\Delta z_j$, retain a vector of cash-flow or key-rate sensitivities and its covariance. Duration compresses their sum into one direction. A twist can hurt a portfolio with zero parallel duration, as the explicit counterexample in Chapter 9 shows. Credit spreads, inflation, optional payments, and financing are additional factors when the instrument requires them.

## 3. Cash-flow mapping preserves chosen statistics, not the original claim [17:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xuUX0FVxwv0&t=1046s)

The lecture maps an off-grid payment into positions at neighboring benchmark maturities so available covariance data can be used. This is a risk-model approximation. Two payments at different dates do not automatically replicate one payment between them.

Its example pays 50,000 at 0.3 years. Neighboring annual effective spot rates are 5.5% at 0.25 years and 6% at 0.5 years. Linear interpolation in the *rate* gives 5.6%, hence present value

$$B_X=50000(1.056)^{-0.3}=49189.321135.$$

Interpolating zero rates, log discount factors, or discount factors would generally give different off-grid prices. Choosing one is part of the model, not a theorem of no-arbitrage from only two observations.

The benchmark daily return volatilities are $\sigma_A=0.0006$, $\sigma_B=0.0010$, correlation 0.9. Interpolating the volatility at 0.3 years gives $\sigma_X=0.00068$. To allocate fraction $w$ of the *present value* to A and $1-w$ to B while matching that assumed variance, solve

$$w^2\sigma_A^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_B^2
+2w(1-w)\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B=\sigma_X^2.$$

There is one root in $[0,1]$, $w=0.760258938$. The allocations are 37396.621030 and 11792.700105. Their sum matches present value, and their modeled variance matches the interpolated target. The other quadratic root is outside the stipulated nonnegative-allocation interval. In another example there could be two feasible roots or none; a solver must inspect the feasible set rather than take whichever root appears first.

These are present-value allocations, not face amounts. To obtain quantities of benchmark zero bonds, divide each allocation by that benchmark's price per unit face. Nor does this construction preserve every rate sensitivity: its present-value-weighted maturity is approximately $0.25w+0.5(1-w)=0.3099353$ years, rather than 0.3. A method designed to match present value and variance should not be described as exact duration matching as well. The target variance itself was interpolated, not measured for the off-grid claim. Mapping error must remain visible in the risk assessment.

### Matching one claim's variance need not preserve portfolio variance

There is a further boundary even if the off-grid variance were known perfectly. Let $X$ be the original claim's monetary value change, $M$ its mapped change, and $Y$ the rest of the portfolio's value change. Assume all three have finite second moments. Expanding both variances gives

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y+M)-\operatorname{Var}(Y+X)
=\operatorname{Var}(M)-\operatorname{Var}(X)
+2\operatorname{Cov}(Y,M-X).$$

Matching the individual variances removes only the first difference. It does not remove the covariance term. For a deliberately simple counterexample, take independent symmetric signs $Z,W\in\{-1,1\}$ and a monetary scale $s>0$, then set $X=sZ$, $M=sW$, and $Y=-sZ$. The original portfolio change $Y+X$ is zero in every state, but the mapped change $Y+M=s(W-Z)$ has variance $2s^2$, although $X$ and $M$ each have variance $s^2$. This abstract moment example is not a claim about the recording's particular interpolation data. It demonstrates exactly what extra information is missing from a standalone variance match.

Thus a portfolio mapping policy must also specify how the off-grid claim's covariance with other positions is represented or approximated. Comparing the resulting risk against richer curve scenarios tests that approximation. Present-value matching and a good single-claim volatility are useful checks, but they are not a complete portfolio-risk certificate.

## 4. Principal components compress factor variance, not every portfolio's risk [22:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xuUX0FVxwv0&t=1357s)

For centered factor changes with symmetric covariance $\Sigma$, write an orthogonal eigendecomposition $\Sigma=U\Lambda U^\mathsf{T}$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_j\ge0$. Principal-component changes are $Y=U^\mathsf{T}X$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(Y)=\Lambda$. They are uncorrelated in that covariance model; they need not be independent unless stronger distributional assumptions apply. This is the eigenstructure underlying the [NIST principal-component treatment](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section5/pmc551.htm), particularly its orthogonal-transform and diagonal-covariance equations.

The portfolio's transformed exposures are $b=U^\mathsf{T}a$, giving

$$\sigma_L^2=\sum_j b_j^2\lambda_j.$$

Keeping only the largest $k$ eigenvalues discards portfolio variance $\sum_{j>k}b_j^2\lambda_j$ in this linear covariance calculation. A high percentage of *factor* variance retained, $\sum_{j\le k}\lambda_j/\sum_j\lambda_j$, does not guarantee a high percentage of this portfolio's variance retained. Exposures matter as well as eigenvalues.

For an exact two-factor counterexample, let

$$\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.0001&0.000099\\0.000099&0.0001\end{pmatrix},
\qquad a=(10^6,-10^6)^\mathsf{T}.$$

The large eigenvalue 0.000199 explains 99.5% of total factor variance and points along $(1,1)$. Our portfolio is orthogonal to it. Its entire variance, 2,000,000 currency units squared, comes from the small eigenvalue 0.000001 in the $(1,-1)$ direction. Truncating to the dominant component would report zero linear risk for a position whose standard deviation is 1414.213562. PCA can be valuable without that truncation being appropriate for this exposure. Standardizing factors before PCA also changes the optimization; covariance-PCA and correlation-PCA are different modeling choices.

The source's separate two-component example assumes factor standard deviations 20 and eight with exposures six and −four. Uncorrelatedness gives variance $6^2(20^2)+(-4)^2(8^2)=15424$, standard deviation 124.193398, and five-day 90% Gaussian VaR 355.893123 using the precise quantile. The source's rounded 1.28 gives about 355.46. Those factors carry their specified units; they are not automatically percentage returns or decimal yields merely because they came from a PCA routine.

## 5. A currency forward contains two oppositely signed bond exposures [34:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xuUX0FVxwv0&t=2078s)

Let $X$ be domestic currency per foreign unit. An existing forward receives $N_f$ foreign units and pays fixed $N_d$ domestic units at $T$. Under the matched deterministic-curve assumptions of Chapter 2, its current domestic value is

$$V=N_fXP_f(0,T)-N_dP_d(0,T)=A-B.$$

The foreign payment's current domestic value $A$ uses the current exchange rate. The domestic obligation's notional $N_d$ stays fixed; replacing it by today's exchange rate would change the original contract. A small-return covariance calculation therefore uses signed exposures $(A,-B)$ to the domestic-valued foreign zero bond and domestic zero bond.

For a synthetic example take $N_f=10^6$, $N_d=1.5\times10^6$, $X=1.53$, $P_f=0.975$, and $P_d=0.97$. Then $A=1491750$, $B=1455000$, and current forward value is 36,750. If their daily return volatilities are 0.0006 and 0.0005 with correlation 0.8, monetary standard deviation is 537.152262. Using two positive legs would incorrectly give 1540.197699. Using only the much smaller net value as exposure would also lose the gross risks.

The foreign bond's domestic-valued return already includes exchange-rate risk. Adding the same FX factor a second time would double count it. Alternatively, model local foreign rates, domestic rates, and FX separately, deriving the corresponding gradient and joint covariance. The lecture's closing example supplies the two-bond architecture; its caption omits some exact discount inputs, so these clearly synthetic factors are specified rather than inferred from rounded displayed leg values.

## 6. Delta–gamma loss is generally not normally distributed [32:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xuUX0FVxwv0&t=1962s)

With sufficient differentiability and small factor changes, write

$$L\approx -a^\mathsf{T}X-\tfrac12X^\mathsf{T}HX,$$

where $H$ is the Hessian of value in the *same factor coordinates*. A cash/elapsed-time term can be added when the experiment includes it. For absolute stock increments, the second derivative is gamma. For log-return increments, the one-stock second derivative is $S^2\Gamma+S\Delta$, not $S^2\Gamma$ alone. A Taylor expansion must differentiate the chosen coordinates consistently.

Even Gaussian factors produce a quadratic form whose distribution is generally nonnormal. For centered Gaussian $X$ and a loss written $c+b^\mathsf{T}X+\tfrac12X^\mathsf{T}AX$ with symmetric $A$,

$$E[L]=c+\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma),\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(L)=b^\mathsf{T}\Sigma b+
\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma A\Sigma).$$

The expectation follows from $E[X_iX_j]=\Sigma_{ij}$. The Gaussian fourth-moment identity is $E[X_iX_jX_kX_l]=\Sigma_{ij}\Sigma_{kl}+\Sigma_{ik}\Sigma_{jl}+\Sigma_{il}\Sigma_{jk}$, obtained by differentiating the moment-generating function $\exp(t^\mathsf{T}\Sigma t/2)$ four times at zero. Substitution into the expanded quadratic square gives the trace variance above; centered odd moments make the linear–quadratic covariance zero. These moments do not make a Gaussian quantile formula exact for the quadratic loss. The lab checks a one-factor quadratic's moments by Gaussian quadrature, separately from computing finite-scenario quantiles.

Consider a synthetic short position in 100 calls, hedged by buying its initial delta in stock. The model inputs are spot and strike 100, rate 2%, volatility 20%, remaining maturity 0.25 year. For an *instantaneous stock-only* stress hold time, volatility, and rates fixed; no elapsed-time or funding cash flow occurs. Its loss at stock increment $x$ is

$$L(x)=100[c(100+x)-c(100)-\Delta x].$$

The linear approximation is identically zero, but convexity makes exact loss nonnegative and positive away from zero. Use increments $(-20,-10,0,10,20)$ with stated probabilities $(0.01,0.09,0.80,0.09,0.01)$. These are an original discrete scenario distribution, not a fitted market model.

| Stock increment | Probability | Linear loss | Gamma loss | Full repricing loss |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| −20 | 0.01 | 0 | 793.905095 | 661.031050 |
| −10 | 0.09 | 0 | 198.476274 | 194.852544 |
| 0 | 0.80 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 10 | 0.09 | 0 | 198.476274 | 173.412792 |
| 20 | 0.01 | 0 | 793.905095 | 559.936891 |

Exact option repricing gives 95% VaR 194.852544 and expected shortfall 361.105115. A gamma-only calculation gives VaR 198.476274 and ES 436.647802; linear VaR is zero. The missing loss arises from curvature, while the gamma error reflects the finite shock and changing gamma.

For a separate five-year zero bond priced at continuous yield 4%, a 100-basis-point yield rise gives exact loss 39929.970007 per million face. Duration estimates 40936.537654; duration plus convexity estimates 39913.124213. The second-order estimate improves this example. It is not guaranteed to improve every derivative or large shock: Taylor remainders, changed exercise, omitted factors, and nonsmooth payoffs must be examined. Full repricing within a model is a benchmark for its approximation, not proof that the model captures actual tail behavior.

## 7. Quantiles and tail averages answer different questions

Chapter 23 defines expected shortfall through the upper quantile integral, which remains valid with atoms:

$$\operatorname{ES}_{\alpha}(L)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
\int_{\alpha}^{1}\operatorname{VaR}_u(L)\,du.$$

For a finite distribution, sort losses and accumulate the worst $1-\alpha$ probability mass, using only the needed fraction of a boundary atom. The algorithm does not simply average all losses satisfying $L\ge\operatorname{VaR}_{\alpha}$, nor does it always equal the conditional mean above a strict threshold. The general-distribution formulation is developed by [Rockafellar and Uryasev, Definition 3 and Proposition 8, PDF pages 6–8 and 13](https://sites.math.washington.edu/~rtr/papers/rtr187-CVaR2.pdf#page=6); the local computation here follows directly from the quantile integral.

For losses $(0,10,100)$ with probabilities $(0.90,0.08,0.02)$, 95% VaR is ten. The worst 5% comprises all 2% at 100 and only 3% of the 8% atom at ten, so ES is $(0.02(100)+0.03(10))/0.05=46$. Conditioning on losses strictly greater than ten instead gives 100, a different quantity. For a separate loss that is zero with probability 0.99 and 1,000 with probability 0.01, 95% VaR is zero while ES is 200. A zero quantile does not establish zero exposure to severe loss.

Finite scenarios must carry justified probabilities for VaR/ES to be probabilistic estimates. A stress test can specify a severe shock without assigning it an estimated likelihood. Both are useful, but a stress loss cannot be given a calibrated confidence level merely because it was placed in a scenario table.

## 8. Backtesting tests a forecast against matching outcomes

At each date compute a risk forecast using information available then, and later compare it with the matching loss. Define an exception as $I_t=\mathbf1\{L_t>\operatorname{VaR}_{\alpha,t}\}$. With a correctly calibrated continuous conditional distribution, the conditional exception probability is $1-\alpha$. At a quantile atom, strict exceedance probability can be smaller, so testing exact equality needs an appropriate treatment of ties; the discrete examples above should not be fed into a continuous-calibration test without that adjustment.

Under the additional independent Bernoulli assumption with common probability $p=1-\alpha$, total exceptions over $n$ dates have the binomial law. At 99% over 250 dates, the expected count is 2.5 and probability of zero exceptions is $0.99^{250}=0.08105852$. Thus an exception does not automatically falsify the model, and no exceptions do not establish perfection. The probability of ten or more is approximately 0.00025019 under this particular null model, not a universal probability for market outcomes.

A count also discards time ordering. Ten consecutive exceptions and ten widely separated exceptions have the same total but different clustering. Examine conditional performance, changing volatility, overlapping horizons, and the size and cause of losses. With overlapping multi-day forecasts, independence of exception indicators is generally unavailable. A historically fitted model evaluated on the same observations provides weaker evidence than genuinely subsequent forecasts.

Match the P&L definition too. A static-position risk calculation should be compared with a hypothetical outcome using those same positions; actual trading P&L can additionally contain new trades, fees, and other income. Both can be informative when their differences are understood. The [Basel Committee's 1996 backtesting note, PDF pages 2–6](https://www.bis.org/publications/199601-standards-supervisory-framework-use-backtesting-conjunction-internal-models-approach-market-risk-capital.pdf#page=2), explicitly discusses this comparison and the statistical limitations of exception counts. This is a historical methodological reference, superseded as a regulatory standard; no current capital-rule certification is inferred from our binomial example.
