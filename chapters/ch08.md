# Discount factors price cash flows; a yield summarizes one price

A bond pays at several dates, while a yield quotation compresses its price into one number. That compression is useful for quoting and comparison, but it discards information about the term structure. Two bonds valued on the same discount curve can have different yields to maturity because their payment patterns differ. Conversely, different discount curves can give the same price and yield for one bond.

The right starting object is therefore a dated cash flow and its discount factor. We will derive spot and forward rates from discount factors, distinguish coupon rate from yield, solve the nonlinear yield equation, and separate a bond's internal rate of return from the investor's eventual compounded wealth. This distinction becomes indispensable when duration is used to measure a bond's sensitivity to a quoted yield.

## 1. Define discounting by the payment being purchased [02:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EEG2kXFqpKk&t=140s)

Let $P(0,t)>0$ be the time-zero price of a default-free claim paying one currency unit at date $t>0$, with no other payments. It is dimensionless when expressed as currency paid today per unit of the same currency at maturity. Assume payments have the same currency, credit quality, and funding/collateral setting so a common discount curve applies. Promised risky corporate payments generally cannot be priced by mechanically inserting a default-free curve without addressing credit and other contractual risks.

The annual effective spot rate $z_t>-1$ and continuously compounded spot rate $r_t$ are alternative encodings of the same discount factor:

$$P(0,t)=(1+z_t)^{-t}=e^{-r_tt},\qquad
r_t=\frac{-\log P(0,t)}{t},\quad z_t=e^{r_t}-1.$$

Time $t$ is a specified year fraction. Annual, semiannual nominal, simple money-market, and continuously compounded quotations are not interchangeable numerical inputs. A rate can be negative while the discount factor remains positive; positivity alone does not require $P(0,t)\le1$. We do not assume positive rates as a theorem of no-arbitrage.

For deterministic cash payments $C_k$ at dates $t_k$, replication by dated unit claims gives the full present value

$$B_0=\sum_{k=1}^{N}C_kP(0,t_k).$$

Here $B_0$ denotes the bond's full cash-flow value, keeping $P$ available for discount factors. The equality follows from matching each payment date, not from averaging rates. If the traded bond and its replicating claims have different prices, buying the cheaper payment stream and selling the dearer gives the statewise price discrepancy under frictionless executable trading. [MIT's fixed-income notes, slides 25–34](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-401-finance-theory-i-fall-2008/df418b972d36cd53ae5c375b8af61e53_MIT15_401F08_lec04.pdf#page=25), explain the coupon bond as a portfolio of dated zero-coupon claims.

The coupon rate is contractual: a conventional annual coupon $C=cM$ is based on face amount $M$ and coupon rate $c$. It is not the return on the price currently paid. A bond selling above face can have a coupon rate above its yield; the premium paid for its larger coupons contributes to that difference. Payments between coupon dates also require the clean/dirty-price and accrued-interest conventions developed in Chapter 11.

## 2. Forward rates are locked ratios, not guaranteed forecasts

Take dates $0<u<v$ and suppose the relevant dated claims can be traded. A forward agreement to lend one unit at $u$ and receive $A$ at $v$ has zero initial value precisely when

$$-P(0,u)+AP(0,v)=0,\qquad A=\frac{P(0,u)}{P(0,v)}.$$

The payment stream is replicated by shorting one $u$-maturity unit claim and buying $A$ $v$-maturity claims; their initial costs cancel. A lender already expecting one unit at $u$ can offset that short payment and lock the later receipt. This dated-claim argument is the bridge to forward-rate agreements later in the book.

For a simple forward rate over accrual fraction $\delta$, write $A=1+f_{u,v}\delta$, giving

$$f_{u,v}=\frac{P(0,u)/P(0,v)-1}{\delta}.$$

For continuous compounding, $f^c_{u,v}=\log[P(0,u)/P(0,v)]/(v-u)$. These are two quotations of the same locked accumulation factor when the time conventions align. They are not generally equal to the future spot rate that will actually prevail at $u$. Equating a current forward with an expected future spot rate needs additional assumptions about risk premiums and the probability measure. [MIT's slides 19–24](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-401-finance-theory-i-fall-2008/df418b972d36cd53ae5c375b8af61e53_MIT15_401F08_lec04.pdf#page=19) make the future transaction dates and replication explicit.

For annual effective spot rates $z_1=0.10$ and $z_2=0.15$, $P(0,1)=1/1.10$ and $P(0,2)=1/1.15^2$. The locked one-year factor from year one to year two is $1.15^2/1.10\approx1.2022727$, so the one-year forward rate is about 20.2273%. It exceeds both spot rates because the two-year rate combines a relatively low first-year accumulation with a higher locked second-year accumulation. Calling the two-year spot rate “the rate earned in year two” would confuse these objects.

### Recovering discount factors from coupon-bond prices

When earlier discount factors are known, a later coupon bond can reveal the next one. For an annual two-year bond with payments $C$ and $C+M$,

$$P(0,2)=\frac{B_0-CP(0,1)}{C+M}.$$

This is a simple **bootstrap**: solve sequentially from prices rather than assigning every payment the bond's own quoted yield. As a synthetic example, $P(0,1)=0.95$, $C=3$, $M=100$, and $B_0=98$ imply $P(0,2)\approx0.9237864$. Positivity is a necessary check. Real instruments have irregular dates, differing coupon schedules, bid/ask prices, and interpolation choices; one equation does not establish a complete continuous curve.

## 3. Yield to maturity is a nonlinear inverse for one payment stream [04:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EEG2kXFqpKk&t=240s)

For annual payments at integer years, the annual effective yield to maturity $y_b$ is defined by

$$B_0=\Phi(y_b):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{C_k}{(1+y_b)^k},\qquad y_b>-1.$$

For positive payments and positive price, $\Phi$ is continuous, strictly decreasing, tends to infinity as $y_b\downarrow-1$, and tends to zero as $y_b\to\infty$. Indeed,

$$\Phi'(y_b)=-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{kC_k}{(1+y_b)^{k+1}}<0.$$

Thus there is exactly one root. This supplies the mathematical justification for a bracketed numerical solver. Cash streams with both positive and negative future payments can have multiple internal rates or no admissible rate; uniqueness must not be exported to them without proof. Irregular dates need an explicitly specified yield convention, including the annualization and coupon frequency.

For a zero-coupon bond paying $M$ at $N$, the yield is $(M/B_0)^{1/N}-1$, exactly the spot rate for that maturity and convention. For a positive coupon stream priced from spot rates $z_k$, its YTM lies between their minimum and maximum over the actual payment dates. To prove this, discount every positive payment at the minimum spot: each resulting present value is at least its curve-discounted value. Using the maximum gives the reverse inequality. Monotonicity of $\Phi$ places the equal-price yield between the two. This result is not a simple arithmetic-average formula.

With the 10%/15% spot curve, a two-year face-1,000 zero costs about 756.143667 and has yield 15%. The annual coupon that makes a two-year bond sell at par solves

$$C\{P(0,1)+P(0,2)\}+1000P(0,2)=1000.$$

It is about 146.4396. For a conventional bond at par, annual coupon rate and annual effective YTM coincide, so its yield is about 14.6440%. A two-payment equal annuity priced at 1,000 instead pays about 600.516 on each date and has a lower YTM, nearer the earlier spot rate. These source-inspired cases use the same curve but different cash-flow weights. The lab prints the precise values rather than relying on rounded slide numbers.

For a conventional level-coupon bond with annual coupon rate $c$ and redemption at face, evaluating $\Phi(c)$ gives face value by the annuity identity. Since $\Phi$ decreases, a price above face implies $y_b<c$, and a price below face implies $y_b>c$. This comparison needs the stated schedule and convention. It does not say that every security quoting above an arbitrary reference amount has yield below its nominal coupon.

## 4. One bond's yield cannot identify the whole curve [21:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EEG2kXFqpKk&t=1280s)

A two-payment stream gives one price equation in two discount factors:

$$B_0=C_1P(0,1)+C_2P(0,2).$$

If $C_2\ne0$, choose any admissible first factor and set the second to $(B_0-C_1P(0,1))/C_2$. Many positive pairs can therefore give the same price. The unique YTM for this positive payment stream remains unchanged. Uniqueness of the scalar price–yield inverse is entirely compatible with nonuniqueness of the underlying curve.

Use $C_1=650$, $C_2=575$, and initial spot rates 8% and 12%. Compute the price from that curve, then change the first spot rate to 8.5% and solve for the second discount factor to preserve the price exactly. The resulting second spot is different, while the bond's yield remains the same. This construction avoids attributing exact equality to the source's rounded alternative rates.

There is also no general weighted-average rule for the YTM of a bond portfolio. Combine one bond priced at 100 with annual payments $(15,15,115)$ and another priced at 100 with payments $(6,106,0)$. Their individual yields are 15% and 6%, but the combined price 200 and payments $(21,121,115)$ give a yield about 11.29%, rather than their 10.5% capital-weighted average. The nonlinear discount equation acts on the aggregate payments. Arithmetic portfolio-return linearity concerns returns measured over one common horizon; these different-maturity internal-rate calculations are a different object.

## 5. Reinvestment affects terminal wealth, not whether YTM can be computed [01:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_asG_7J7zs0&t=70s)

The YTM equation is a present-value definition and requires no actual reinvestment transaction. Its use as a terminal compounded growth rate requires an additional condition. Multiplying the annual equation by $(1+y_b)^N$ gives

$$B_0(1+y_b)^N=\sum_{k=1}^{N}C_k(1+y_b)^{N-k}.$$

The right side is the terminal wealth that would result if each payment were reinvested at $y_b$ until maturity. Thus realizing that terminal growth rate requires the relevant reinvestment opportunities and payment performance. It is more precise to distinguish the algebraic definition from this wealth interpretation than to say that computing an internal rate itself assumes that actual coupons have been reinvested.

If all coupons are reinvested at a constant annual rate $j>-1$, terminal wealth is $W_N(j)=\sum_kC_k(1+j)^{N-k}$, and the realized annual compound yield is

$$g_N(j)=\left(\frac{W_N(j)}{B_0}\right)^{1/N}-1.$$

This is a retrospective outcome when the payments and reinvestments have occurred, and a scenario projection when $j$ is an assumption. For positive cash flows held to contractual maturity, $g_N(j)$ lies between $j$ and $y_b$. If $j>y_b$, each accumulation factor at $j$ is at least its counterpart at $y_b$, so $g_N(j)\ge y_b$. Also $B_0=\sum C_k/(1+y_b)^k\ge\sum C_k/(1+j)^k$, which after multiplication by $(1+j)^N$ gives $W_N(j)\le B_0(1+j)^N$, hence $g_N(j)\le j$. Reverse both comparisons for $j<y_b$.

The maturity condition is essential. At an earlier horizon $H$, wealth includes the sale price of the remaining bond as well as accumulated received payments. That sale price can change with the curve, credit quality, liquidity, or embedded options. It can put the realized holding-period yield outside the interval between initial YTM and coupon reinvestment rate. The lecture's interval claim therefore needs this qualification when discussing early liquidation.

A higher YTM also need not dominate terminal wealth under a common reinvestment scenario. Compare two synthetic 15-year face-100 bonds: A pays annual coupons 10 and is priced to yield 6%; B pays coupons 3 and is priced to yield 6.1%. At reinvestment rate 9%, A's larger earlier payments benefit more from reinvestment. Its terminal annual compound yield exceeds B's despite A's lower initial YTM. The lab computes prices from exact face-100 payments so no inconsistent rounded redemption amount is needed. This is a scenario comparison, not a forecast of future reinvestment rates.

## 6. Rate conventions must preserve the actual price [25:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_asG_7J7zs0&t=1510s)

For a bill paying face $M$ in $d$ actual days, a specified bank-discount quotation $b$ uses the face denominator and a 360-day basis:

$$b=\frac{M-B_0}{M}\frac{360}{d},\qquad
B_0=M\left(1-b\frac d{360}\right).$$

This is a quotation convention, not an error in the security's cash flows. The investment holding-period return instead uses $B_0$ in the denominator. An annual effective 365-day equivalent is $(M/B_0)^{365/d}-1$, under that explicitly chosen annualization. For $M=100$, $B_0=99$, and $d=60$, discount yield is 6%, while the effective annual equivalent is about 6.3047%. [TreasuryDirect's pricing explanation](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/understanding-pricing/) confirms the bill price–discount-rate relation. Chapters 10–11 develop the associated futures quotations and historical product conventions.

Run [the laboratory](../labs/ch08.py) to compute the curve prices, solve yields with explicit brackets, verify exact cash-flow repricing, construct an alternative curve with unchanged YTM, and check both portfolio-yield nonlinearity and reinvestment-rank reversal. The solver validates positive cash flows and positive prices. Its convergence establishes an internal-rate calculation for those inputs; it does not make contractual payments certain or guarantee a reinvestment opportunity.
