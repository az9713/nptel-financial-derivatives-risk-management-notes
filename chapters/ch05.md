# Hedge size is a covariance projection with trading units

A processor has a known purchase or inventory quantity but cannot trade a futures contract on the exact material. A related futures price moves with its cash price, yet not one for one. Matching the physical quantity alone can therefore overhedge or underhedge. The right coefficient depends on how their changes covary over the processor's decision horizon, and the final number of exchange contracts must also respect the contract multiplier.

We use a synthetic long inventory exposure of 230,000 units and a related futures contract representing 25,000 futures-underlying units. The horizon standard deviations of price changes are 2 and 2.5 in their respective per-unit quotation systems, with correlation 0.8. We derive the continuous optimum, convert it to contracts, and quantify the additional risk caused by integer rounding. The last step asks what remains valid if the estimated relationship changes.

## 1. Specify the random cash flow before minimizing it [18:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nDESESqRF-s&t=1138s)

Let $Q>0$ be the fixed exposed quantity of asset A. Let $\Delta S$ be its price change over the hedge horizon, in currency per A unit. Let $\Delta G$ be the quotation change of a futures contract on asset B, in currency per B unit. Its multiplier $m>0$ is B units per contract. For a long A exposure hedged with $n_s$ short B contracts, the combined value change is

$$Z(n_s)=Q\Delta S-n_s m\Delta G.$$

We define $n_s$ as a signed **number of short contracts** for this equation. Thus $n_s>0$ means a short hedge and $n_s<0$ means a long hedge. The subscript distinguishes this short count from the signed-long position $n$ used in the futures settlement ledger; the explicit minus sign records the economic direction. The economic position must not be inferred from a coefficient without its sign convention.

Assume finite second moments, a fixed horizon, fixed quantity, and a futures-change variance $\sigma_G^2>0$. Write $\sigma_S^2=\operatorname{Var}(\Delta S)$ and $c=\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S,\Delta G)$. The **correlation coefficient** is $\rho=c/(\sigma_S\sigma_G)$ when both standard deviations are positive. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ensures $|\rho|\le1$.

Our objective is minimum variance of $Z$, not maximum expected profit, minimum margin, or minimum worst-case loss. Fees and financing are omitted from the first sizing calculation. An uncertain quantity $Q$ would make $Q\Delta S$ a product of random variables and require a different exposure model; simply inserting expected quantity would generally omit volume–price dependence.

## 2. Derive the optimum and prove it is global [25:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nDESESqRF-s&t=1500s)

For random variables $A$ and $B$ with finite second moments, expanding their centered square gives $\operatorname{Var}(aA+bB)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(A)+b^2\operatorname{Var}(B)+2ab\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)$ for constant real $a,b$. Applying this identity to the hedged exposure yields

$$v(n_s)=Q^2\sigma_S^2+n_s^2m^2\sigma_G^2-2Qn_s m c.$$

Differentiate with respect to the controllable position:

$$v'(n_s)=2n_s m^2\sigma_G^2-2Qmc.$$

The stationary position and associated unit hedge ratio are

$$n_{s,*} =\frac{Qc}{m\sigma_G^2},\qquad
h_* =\frac{n_{s,*}m}{Q}=\frac{c}{\sigma_G^2}
=\rho\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_G}.$$

The second derivative is $2m^2\sigma_G^2>0$, so the quadratic is strictly convex and $n_{s,*}$ is its unique unconstrained global minimizer. An even more informative proof completes the square:

$$v(n_s)=Q^2\sigma_S^2(1-\rho^2)
+m^2\sigma_G^2(n_s-n_{s,*})^2.$$

The first term is irreducible variance under this single-instrument linear hedge. The second is the exact penalty for sizing away from the continuous optimum. This decomposition proves globality, quantifies rounding costs, and identifies which market relationship a perfect hedge would require.

If the futures change has zero variance, the division formula does not apply. The hedge instrument then supplies no random offset over the modeled horizon. If the exposed change is itself nonrandom, minimizing its variance alone does not justify introducing a risky futures position. Degenerate cases should be handled as such, not hidden by adding a tiny denominator to the formula.

### Units distinguish a hedge ratio from a contract count

The covariance has units currency squared per A-unit per B-unit. Dividing by futures variance, in currency squared per B-unit squared, leaves B units per A unit. Thus $h_*$ is not generally dimensionless in a cross-hedge. Multiplying by $Q$ gives B units; dividing by $m$ gives contracts. A shortcut using the values of the two positions requires a return-based regression and the corresponding price factors. It is not the same formula with names relabeled.

If covariance is negative, $h_*$ and $n_{s,*}$ are negative. Under our definition of $n_s$, the inventory is then hedged by a long futures position. Positive covariance is common in related-commodity illustrations, but it is not part of the minimization theorem.

## 3. The residual is controlled by squared correlation [11:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oFj5lW9QwHc&t=675s)

At the optimum,

$$\operatorname{Var}(Z(n_{s,*}))=Q^2\sigma_S^2(1-\rho^2).$$

The unhedged variance is $Q^2\sigma_S^2$, so the fraction removed is $\rho^2$ and the fraction remaining is $1-\rho^2$. The standard-deviation ratio is $\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$. For $\rho=0.8$, 64% of the modeled variance is removed, 36% remains, and standard deviation is 60% of the unhedged level. Saying that “0.8 correlation hedges 80% and leaves 20% of the fluctuations” conflates these different quantities.

For the running processor, $c=0.8(2)(2.5)=4$ and $h_*=4/6.25=0.64$ B units per A unit. The desired B quantity is 147,200 and $n_{s,*}=147200/25000=5.888$ contracts. Unhedged variance is $230000^2(2^2)=211{,}600{,}000{,}000$ currency units squared. The continuous minimum is 76,176,000,000, with standard deviation 276,000 currency units. These outputs are computed by the chapter lab.

Perfect correlation, including correlation −1, permits zero residual variance in this fixed-horizon model if continuous sizing is feasible. The resulting centered changes are exactly linearly related almost surely. That does not mean zero credit risk, zero margin liquidity need, or zero quantity uncertainty. It also does not imply that a historical sample correlation of one will persist outside the observed sample.

The projection interpretation makes the boundary precise. Regress $\Delta S$ on an intercept and $\Delta G$, obtaining $\Delta S=\alpha+h_*\Delta G+\varepsilon$, with zero covariance between $\Delta G$ and $\varepsilon$. Then $Z(n_{s,*})=Q\alpha+Q\varepsilon$. A fixed intercept can change the expected result while contributing no variance. Zero residual variance makes the result constant; it does not require that constant to be zero.

## 4. Integer contracts create a quantifiable approximation [01:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oFj5lW9QwHc&t=62s)

Exchange trades commonly require integral numbers of contracts. Because the completed-square penalty is proportional to $(n_s-n_{s,*})^2$, the integer variance minimum is attained at an integer nearest to $n_{s,*}$, provided no other constraints or position-dependent costs apply. Evaluate both neighboring integers, $\lfloor n_{s,*}\rfloor$ and $\lfloor n_{s,*}\rfloor+1$. At a half-integer they tie in variance; financing, cost, or exposure preferences can then break the tie under an expanded objective.

For $n_{s,*}=5.888$, six contracts are closer than five. The six-contract position represents 150,000 B units, a little above the continuous target 147,200. Its variance is 76,225,000,000, only 49,000,000 above the continuous minimum. That difference is exactly

$$25000^2(2.5^2)(6-5.888)^2.$$

The small penalty here follows from this exposure scale and curvature, not from a general claim that rounding is harmless. For a small position whose continuous optimum lies between zero and one, one extra contract can materially overhedge it. Contract size is part of risk design, even when the futures price is highly correlated with the exposed price.

Position limits or a prohibition on short positions change the feasible set. For a scalar convex quadratic, project the continuous optimum onto the allowed real interval, then inspect feasible neighboring integers. If costs or funding penalties depend on $n_s$, optimize the expanded objective; nearest-integer variance rounding no longer solves that different problem automatically.

## 5. Cross-hedging leaves two kinds of relative-price uncertainty [20:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oFj5lW9QwHc&t=1205s)

Let $S_H^A$ be the exposed asset's price and $S_H^B$ the spot price of the futures underlying at hedge closure. For a one-for-one unit comparison with aligned quotation units, the combined short-hedge sale price can be written

$$S_H^A+G_0^B-G_H^B
=G_0^B+(S_H^B-G_H^B)+(S_H^A-S_H^B).$$

The first random difference is B's own spot–futures basis. The second is the relative spot-price difference between A and B. Even if B's futures contract reaches convergence, the A-versus-B spread need not vanish. With a general hedge ratio, the corresponding terms are rescaled; the decomposition should not be applied across incompatible physical or currency units.

For currency cross-hedges, first determine which currency the contract pays and how the quotation is expressed. A payable in dollars viewed by a euro-based firm can be hedged with a short position in euro futures quoted in dollars per euro. But its dollar futures gain must be converted to euros consistently with the invoice's valuation date. Dividing the invoice by one exchange rate while leaving the derivative settlement in another currency creates an accounting error masquerading as a hedge result.

The population formula depends on unknown future covariance and variance. A sample estimator substitutes observations over a chosen horizon. The [NIST least-squares reference](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmd/section1/pmd141.htm) defines fitting through squared deviations and emphasizes extrapolation and outlier sensitivity. In hedging, these limitations become uncertainty about the future exposure coefficient. Using more observations does not repair a sample that measures the wrong maturity, currency, or economic regime.

### A fitted optimum can become harmful after a relationship changes

Take four equally weighted futures changes $x=(-2,-1,1,2)$ and residuals $e=(1,-1,-1,1)$. The residual mean and its covariance with $x$ are zero. A training relationship $\Delta S=0.8x+e$ therefore has minimum-variance coefficient 0.8 and residual variance 1. Suppose the future relationship becomes $\Delta S=-0.2x+e$ while the hedge remains at 0.8. The resulting residual is $-x+e$, with variance 3.5, whereas the new unhedged variance is only 1.1.

This is a constructed counterexample to a universal guarantee, not a statistical estimate of the likelihood of regime change. It proves why an in-sample hedge optimum is conditional on the relationship being modeled. Stressing the coefficient, examining different horizons, and evaluating genuinely separate data address different aspects of that uncertainty. None converts a covariance estimate into a statewise guarantee.
