# Duration cancels a first derivative, not every interest-rate risk

A five-year bond paying a 12% annual coupon is priced at a yield of 18%. What happens if its yield rises to 25%? Full repricing answers exactly for the stipulated cash flows. Duration gives a local linear estimate; convexity supplies a second-order correction. The difference between these answers shows both why sensitivities are useful and why a large rate shock should not be treated as an infinitesimal change.

The same derivative logic explains immunization. A carefully selected holding horizon can make coupon-reinvestment gains offset sale-price losses to first order under a common-rate model. Matching asset and liability duration can similarly cancel the first derivative of surplus. Neither statement eliminates curve twists, changing credit spreads, cash-flow uncertainty, or the need to rebalance.

## 1. State which rate moves and which cash flows remain fixed [05:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pu7ynYUrDDg&t=310s)

Use annual payments $C_k\ge0$ at integer years $k=1,\ldots,N$, with at least one positive payment. Let $y_b>-1$ be the annual effective yield and define full price

$$B(y_b)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}C_k(1+y_b)^{-k}.$$

For this section the contractual payments remain fixed when the yield changes. This matters: a callable bond's exercise-dependent payments or a floating-rate coupon schedule can change with the rate environment. They require a fuller model, and the positive fixed-cash-flow formulas need not describe their effective sensitivities.

Differentiation gives

$$B'(y_b)=-\sum_k kC_k(1+y_b)^{-k-1}<0,\qquad
B''(y_b)=\sum_k k(k+1)C_k(1+y_b)^{-k-2}>0.$$

The first derivative is negative and the price–yield graph is convex. These signs are properties of this mathematical pricing function; they do not require an assertion that only unanticipated economic news can change a bond's observed price. Price changes can also reflect the passage of time, distributions, and changes in other inputs.

Convexity implies that the tangent line lies below the true curve. For an admissible equal-sized yield rise and fall, the price gain on the fall exceeds the absolute loss on the rise: $B(y_b-h)+B(y_b+h)>2B(y_b)$ for $h>0$ with $y_b-h>-1$. This is an exact convexity statement. It does not mean that a convex bond is free of downside or that every security with a fixed-income label has positive effective convexity.

## 2. Duration is a present-value time average and a sensitivity [11:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pu7ynYUrDDg&t=680s)

Define positive present-value weights $\omega_k=C_k(1+y_b)^{-k}/B(y_b)$, so $\sum_k\omega_k=1$. The **Macaulay duration** is

$$D_{\rm Mac}=\sum_k k\omega_k.$$

It is a weighted average payment time in years under this annual schedule. Thus it lies between the earliest and latest positive payment dates. A zero-coupon bond has duration exactly equal to maturity. A coupon bond with a positive early payment has duration below its final payment time. This average is a mathematical weighting of payments, not the calendar date at which nominal receipts necessarily recover the purchase price.

Using the derivative above,

$$D_{\rm mod}:=-\frac{B'}{B}=\frac{D_{\rm Mac}}{1+y_b},\qquad
\frac{\Delta B}{B}\approx-D_{\rm mod}\Delta y_b.$$

The quoted yield increment is a decimal rate increment: a rise from 5% to 6% means $\Delta y_b=0.01$, while one basis point means $10^{-4}$. For a long positive bond define the positive local dollar-value-per-basis-point magnitude

$$\mathrm{DV01}=-B'(y_b)10^{-4}
=BD_{\rm mod}10^{-4}.$$

For a position containing several bonds, scale by the signed number held. A derivative $-B'$ alone is currency per unit decimal yield, not currency per basis point. The lecture's verbal DV01 identification omits this conversion when the differentiation variable is a decimal annual rate. If a quotation instead differentiates with respect to basis-point units, the factor is already implicit; always state the rate coordinate.

The signed elasticity is $y_bB'/B=-y_bD_{\rm mod}$ for nonzero $y_b$, representing percentage price sensitivity per percentage yield change. Near zero yield, relative changes in the yield are a poor coordinate even though $B'$, modified duration, and DV01 remain well defined. Absolute basis-point changes are usually easier to interpret.

For nominal annual yield $y_b$ compounded $a$ times per year, payment dates $t_k=k/a$ give

$$B=\sum_k C_k(1+y_b/a)^{-k},\quad
D_{\rm Mac}=\sum_k t_k\omega_k,\quad
D_{\rm mod}=\frac{D_{\rm Mac}}{1+y_b/a}.$$

The factors of $a$ come from differentiating with respect to the annual nominal yield; using the per-period yield as the derivative coordinate changes the numerical derivative. [MIT's fixed-income slides 35–43](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-401-finance-theory-i-fall-2008/df418b972d36cd53ae5c375b8af61e53_MIT15_401F08_lec04.pdf#page=35) develop duration, compounding, and the second-order approximation.

## 3. Convexity quantifies the leading departure from the tangent [25:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pu7ynYUrDDg&t=1545s)

Define normalized yield convexity by

$$\mathcal C=\frac{B''(y_b)}{B(y_b)}
=\frac{\sum_k k(k+1)\omega_k}{(1+y_b)^2}.$$

Our symbol includes the yield-denominator factor and excludes the Taylor coefficient $1/2$. Some sources incorporate one of these factors into their definition. Comparing numerical convexities without comparing formulas can create a factor-of-two or compounding error. With this definition,

$$\frac{\Delta B}{B}=-D_{\rm mod}\Delta y_b
+\frac12\mathcal C(\Delta y_b)^2+O((\Delta y_b)^3).$$

For a finite fixed payment schedule the function is smooth on $y_b>-1$. Taylor's theorem gives an exact remainder $B'''(\xi)(\Delta y_b)^3/(6B(y_b))$ for some intermediate $\xi$, as long as the whole interval stays in that domain. Here $B'''<0$. For a positive yield shock the quadratic approximation overstates the true price and the tangent understates it. The second-order term often improves accuracy, but a general claim that each additional Taylor term always improves every large-shock estimate would require more control.

For the opening face-100 bond, payments are $(12,12,12,12,112)$. The lab computes price, duration, convexity, and both approximations at 18%, then reprices at 25%. Price starts near 81.237 and falls near 65.04; the precise numbers are printed by the lab. The duration-only estimated decline is larger than the actual decline, and the positive convexity correction moves the estimate toward the true price. This independently recomputes the lecture's illustrative large-shock case rather than treating its rounded 81.224 and 65 figures as exact inputs.

We can bound the remaining error rather than merely observe that it is smaller. For this fixed positive payment stream,

$$B'''(y)=-\sum_k k(k+1)(k+2)C_k(1+y)^{-k-3}.$$

For a positive shock $h$, the magnitude $-B'''(y)$ decreases with $y$. If $Q_2$ denotes the second-order price estimate, Taylor's intermediate-point remainder therefore gives

$$\frac{-B'''(y_b+h)}{6}h^3
\le Q_2-B(y_b+h)\le\frac{-B'''(y_b)}{6}h^3.$$

At $y_b=0.18$ and $h=0.07$, these bounds are $0.260714$ and $0.408113$ per face-100 bond. The actual quadratic overstatement, approximately $0.365477$, falls between them. This is a uniform finite-interval certificate under the fixed-payment model, stronger than checking a few nearby prices. It also makes the limitation concrete: even the upper bound grows cubically in the shock and depends on derivatives across an admissible rate interval.

Convexity is a normalized second derivative in a financial rate coordinate. The Euclidean curvature of the plotted curve is $|B''|/[1+(B')^2]^{3/2}$ after choosing compatible coordinate scales. These are not the same quantity: rescaling the axes changes the visual geometry. The lecture's geometric discussion motivates the second derivative, but practical price approximation uses the explicitly defined derivatives above.

## 4. A duration horizon balances reinvestment and sale-price effects [06:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WGKvJQCY__0&t=360s)

Suppose the curve is flat at annual effective rate $y_b$, then undergoes an immediate small parallel change and stays at the new common rate. Assume the same rate applies both to coupon reinvestment and the discounting of the remaining bond at sale, payments remain fixed, and trading is frictionless. At a holding horizon $H$ years, accumulated received coupons plus the value of remaining payments combine into

$$W_H(y_b)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}C_k(1+y_b)^{H-k}
=(1+y_b)^H B(y_b).$$

Positive exponents accumulate earlier payments; negative exponents value later ones at the sale date. This compact identity works for a fractional horizon under the stipulated fractional-year compounding model as well as for integer coupon dates, with sale timing defined consistently.

Differentiate with $H$ fixed:

$$\frac{W_H'}{W_H}=\frac{H-D_{\rm Mac}(y_b)}{1+y_b}.$$

At the initial rate $y_0$, choose $H=D_{\rm Mac}(y_0)$. The first derivative is then zero. A small rate rise improves coupon reinvestment but lowers sale proceeds; their first-order contributions cancel at that horizon. A derivative vanishing at one point means local stationarity, not independence from the rate over an interval.

The second derivative clarifies what remains. Differentiating the present-value weights gives

$$\frac{dD_{\rm Mac}}{dy_b}
=-\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\omega}(k)}{1+y_b}.$$

At the matched horizon, therefore,

$$\frac{W_H''(y_0)}{W_H(y_0)}
=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\omega}(k)}{(1+y_0)^2}\ge0.$$

For a genuine multi-date payment stream the variance is positive, giving a local minimum of terminal wealth in this common-rate model. For a zero maturing exactly at $H$, payment-time variance is zero and terminal wealth is the fixed redemption itself. These conclusions identify the mathematical source of the flat region shown in the lectures. They do not establish immunity to a sequence of independent nonparallel rate changes.

The same weight derivative shows duration decreases as the yield rises for positive fixed payments, strictly when payment times differ. Holding maturity and yield fixed, raising the level coupon relative to redemption shifts present value toward earlier dates and reduces duration for an ordinary coupon bond. Increasing maturity usually increases duration, but sufficiently discounted coupon bonds can show nonmonotonic behavior as their duration approaches the perpetuity limit $(1+y_b)/y_b$ for $y_b>0$. “Longer maturity always means longer duration” is therefore too strong.

## 5. Asset–liability matching controls surplus derivatives

Let $A(y_b)$ be asset value and $L(y_b)$ liability present value under the same scalar yield coordinate. The surplus is $S=A-L$. At a reference rate, matching present values and modified durations gives

$$A=L,\quad D_A=D_L
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S=0,\quad S'=-AD_A+LD_L=0.$$

More generally, first-order dollar sensitivity matches when $AD_A=LD_L$, even if the initial surplus is not zero. If present values match, the local second-order surplus term is $\tfrac12(A\mathcal C_A-L\mathcal C_L)(\Delta y_b)^2$. Sufficiently greater asset convexity creates a favorable local quadratic cushion in this restricted scalar model. It does not remove liquidity demands or establish a global guarantee for arbitrary cash flows and rate paths.

With continuous zero rates, $A=\sum_k A_ke^{-z_kt_k}$. If every $z_k$ is shifted by the same small $\delta$, the corresponding duration is $D_c=\sum t_kA_ke^{-z_kt_k}/A$, with $\partial A/\partial\delta=-AD_c$. Macaulay's extra $1+y_b$ conversion disappears because the derivative coordinate is now a continuous rate. A hedge ratio must use sensitivities with the same coordinate and rate scale.

As an original synthetic construction, put asset present value 50 into a one-year zero and 50 into a three-year zero on a flat continuously compounded 5% curve. Their face amounts are $50e^{0.05}$ and $50e^{0.15}$. The liability is a two-year payment with present value 100, face $100e^{0.10}$. Assets and liability have equal present values and continuous parallel-shift duration two. For a common shift $\delta$, surplus is

$$50e^{-\delta}+50e^{-3\delta}-100e^{-2\delta}
=100e^{-2\delta}(\cosh\delta-1)\ge0.$$

This particular payment arrangement has an exact nonnegative surplus for common shifts, stronger than its local first-order statement. It follows from the specified two-zero construction and common shift, not from duration matching alone.

Now increase the one-year zero rate by 1%, decrease the three-year rate by 1%, and leave the two-year rate unchanged. Asset value becomes $50e^{-0.01}+50e^{0.03}$, while liability stays 100. Reversing the twist gives $50e^{0.01}+50e^{-0.03}<100$. The same duration-matched portfolio can then have a negative surplus. A scalar parallel sensitivity cannot cancel both distinct maturity-specific directions. Their statistical correlation does not alter this algebraic mismatch.

## 6. A curve is a vector of risk coordinates

For maturity-specific continuous zero-rate shocks $\Delta z_k$ at the payment dates, first-order price change is

$$\Delta B\approx-\sum_k t_kC_ke^{-z_kt_k}\Delta z_k.$$

The gradient contains a separate dollar sensitivity for each date. Parallel duration sums these components; that sum loses the information about which maturities carry the risk. Key-rate methods choose a finite set of curve coordinates and a specified interpolation rule, then measure sensitivity to each coordinate. Their values depend on that choice and must be used consistently between assets, liabilities, and hedge instruments.

Even a perfect first-order match for chosen curve coordinates leaves second-order exposure, interpolation/model error, changing cash flows, and any spread or credit risks outside those coordinates. Duration also changes as time passes and coupons are paid, so a portfolio initially matched to a liability generally needs reassessment. A statement about a reference-date derivative is not a permanent property of a trading strategy.
