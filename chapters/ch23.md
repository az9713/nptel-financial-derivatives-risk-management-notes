# Currency swaps, loss quantiles, and tail risk

Currency-swap valuation and value-at-risk answer different questions. Valuation asks what today's contractual cash flows cost under coherent traded prices. VaR asks how a specified portfolio's loss is distributed over a specified future horizon under a chosen risk model. A risk-neutral pricing distribution is not automatically the physical scenario distribution needed for forecasting those losses. The same contract can therefore require one model for conditional valuation and another for the evolution of its risk factors.

The first part of this chapter values currency legs with units attached. The second develops loss quantiles, historical scenarios, Gaussian approximations, and an atom-safe expected-shortfall calculation. The source introduces VaR after currency swaps; expected shortfall and its finite examples are added here to expose exactly what a quantile leaves unmeasured.

## 1. A currency swap needs a quotation direction [06:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t00O7h4AhG8&t=396s)

Let \(X_t\) be domestic currency per one foreign unit. Suppose a swap's remaining foreign receipts are \(C_i^f\) at dates \(T_i\), while domestic payments are \(C_i^d\). All amounts include principal exchanges when the contract requires them. Value the foreign leg in its own currency, \(B_f=\sum_iP_f(t,T_i)C_i^f\), and the domestic leg in domestic currency, \(B_d=\sum_iP_d(t,T_i)C_i^d\). For deterministic cash flows in the ideal frictionless two-currency model,

\[
V_t^d=X_tB_f-B_d.
\]

Units make the conversion unambiguous: domestic/foreign multiplied by foreign gives domestic. If market quotation is foreign per domestic, invert it before using this formula. A notional exchange at inception may already have occurred; do not add it again to a valuation of remaining obligations. In contrast to the same-currency interest swap, equal-looking foreign and domestic principals do not simply cancel at final maturity: their values depend on the exchange rate.

Using covered interest parity, the domestic-per-foreign delivery quote is \(F_i=X_tP_f(t,T_i)/P_d(t,T_i)\). Hence the same value is

\[
V_t^d=\sum_iP_d(t,T_i)[F_iC_i^f-C_i^d].
\]

Each term is a domestic present value of a forward-converted foreign cash flow minus its domestic obligation. Algebra proves equality with the leg method because \(P_dF_i=X_tP_f\). Future spot rates need not actually equal these forward quotes. This is replication using today's conversion prices, not a physical forecast. Different collateral currencies or cross-currency basis conventions require coherent curves and FX forwards; the simple two-risk-free-curve parity model states the assumptions under which this identity is being used.

## 2. Reproduce the yen–dollar swap [14:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t00O7h4AhG8&t=844s)

The source institution receives yen interest of 5% annually on principal 1,200 million yen, and pays dollar interest of 8% annually on 10 million dollars. Three annual payments remain, with principal exchanged at the last date. Current continuous rates are 4% in yen and 9% in dollars, and spot is 110 yen per dollar. Thus \(X=1/110\) dollars per yen.

The dollar payments in millions are 0.8, 0.8, 10.8. Their present value is 9.64385966 million dollars. Yen receipts in millions are 60, 60, 1260; their present value is 1230.55409740 million yen. The receive-yen swap value is therefore

\[
V^\$=\frac{1230.55409740}{110}-9.64385966
=1.54299577\text{ million dollars}.
\]

The laboratory independently forward-converts each yen payment using \(F_i=(1/110)e^{(0.09-0.04)T_i}\), discounts net dollars at 9%, and obtains the same value.

Holding the two curves fixed, current spot delta of this remaining fixed-cash-flow swap is \(\partial_XV=B_f\). It is denominated in foreign units of exposure. The corresponding sensitivity to the inverse quote \(Y=1/X\) is \(-B_f/Y^2\), not the same number with its label changed. A foreign-leg bond can be fixed in foreign currency while its domestic value remains exchange-sensitive.

## 3. Cross-currency funding spreads do not cancel exchange risk [01:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t00O7h4AhG8&t=72s); [08:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t00O7h4AhG8&t=502s)

The recording first extends the comparative-funding exercise. In its single-currency design, A can borrow fixed at 5% or floating at benchmark plus 0.1%; B at 6.4% or benchmark plus 0.6%. The difference between credit spreads is 0.9 percentage points. After an intermediary receives 0.1%, equal distribution of the remaining 0.8% leaves A at benchmark minus 0.3% and B at fixed 6%. These gains are conditional on the contractual and credit qualifications established in Chapter 22.

For a currency example, use matched principals of 10 million US dollars and 20 million Australian dollars at spot 0.5 dollars per Australian dollar. Retain the teaching rates: BBB borrows Australian dollars at 8%, AAA borrows US dollars at 5%, while their desired direct funding would have cost 7% US dollars and 7.6% Australian dollars respectively.

An intermediary can leave BBB paying 6.3% on 10 million US dollars and AAA paying 6.9% on 20 million Australian dollars, canceling their original-currency loan coupons through opposite swap receipts. The intermediary receives a net 130,000 US dollars and pays 220,000 Australian dollars each year. At today's 0.5 conversion this is a 20,000-dollar surplus, but the later domestic value is \(130000-220000X_T\). It is 31,000 at \(X_T=0.45\) and −13,000 at \(X_T=0.65\). A subtraction of percentages in different currencies is not a locked dollar commission. Hedging those residual currency payments or agreeing a different fee allocation has a price. This construction illustrates the units and retained risk; it does not claim to repair unknown slide details or assess an actual funding offer.

## 4. Define the loss before the percentile [23:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t00O7h4AhG8&t=1403s)

Let \(G_h\) be a specified portfolio's gain over horizon \(h\), including whichever carry, cash payments, and trading policy the risk analysis says to include. Define loss \(L_h=-G_h\), so positive loss is adverse. For confidence \(0<\alpha<1\), use the lower quantile convention

\[
\operatorname{VaR}_\alpha(L)=\inf\{\ell:\Pr(L\le\ell)\ge\alpha\}.
\]

The CDF is the cumulative probability \(F_L(\ell)=\Pr(L\le\ell)\). At its VaR threshold \(v\), the strict exceedance probability satisfies \(\Pr(L>v)\le1-\alpha\). It need not equal that number when losses have point masses. VaR is not a maximum possible loss, an expected loss, or an expected loss conditional on a tail event. Losses beyond the threshold may be arbitrarily larger. Increasing confidence cannot decrease the lower loss quantile, but does not always increase it strictly when the CDF jumps.

VaR can be negative when even the relevant adverse percentile is a gain. If loss is always −5, its VaR is −5, not +5. A policy may report \(\max(0,\mathrm{VaR})\), but that is a separately stated reporting convention. Taking the absolute value of a loss quantile would falsely turn guaranteed gains into positive loss exposure.

The source describes confidence as “p percent certain.” Operationally it is a statement conditional on the chosen scenario law and its estimated parameters, not a guarantee that a finite sample will contain exactly that fraction of nonexceedances. A VaR report therefore needs portfolio, horizon, confidence, currency, loss definition, valuation method, and scenario distribution. The distinction between physical risk scenarios and conditional pricing remains essential for derivatives.

## 5. Historical scenarios preserve joint observations [04:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AG4GLF9SBM0&t=273s)

Given consecutive positive historical factor levels \(M_0,\ldots,M_n\), define gross shocks \(g_j=M_j/M_{j-1}\). Apply each to the current factor \(M_*\), giving scenarios \(M_*g_j\), and revalue the current portfolio at the future horizon. The source's 501 levels supply 500 one-day shocks, not 501. Gross ratios are one plus simple returns, rather than the percentage change itself.

For multiple risk factors, retain each date's entire shock vector. Pairing an equity shock from one historical date with an FX shock from another changes dependence and generally changes risk. A portfolio valued in domestic currency can depend on both a foreign asset and its FX conversion. Historical simulation uses the empirical joint scenario distribution without requiring a fitted normal or other parametric family. It does assume that the selected, perhaps weighted historical scenarios are informative about the forecast period. Regime changes, missing joint stresses, stale observations, and illiquid prices can undermine that assumption.

For a transparent construction, hold 100 foreign shares priced at 100 foreign units each, with spot 1.2 domestic per foreign unit. Current domestic value is 12,000. Two joint gross-shock vectors are \((1.1,0.9)\) and \((0.9,1.1)\), where the first coordinate moves stock and the second FX. Both revalued holdings are \(12000(0.99)=11880\), a loss of 120. Reverse only the FX dates and the revaluations become 14,520 and 9,720, producing gain 2,520 or loss 2,280. The marginal shock lists are unchanged, while their pairing and resulting risk differ dramatically. Two scenarios are an illustration of dependence, not a credible high-confidence risk estimate.

For equally likely loss scenarios sorted \(\ell_{(1)}\le\cdots\le\ell_{(n)}\), our quantile is \(\ell_{(\lceil n\alpha\rceil)}\). Thus with 500 losses and 99% confidence, select the 495th in ascending order, the sixth largest. Exactly five observations lie strictly above it when all are distinct. Selecting the fifth worst would use the next order statistic, corresponding to a different empirical quantile convention. Interpolation or strict-CDF quantiles can be chosen, but should not be silently mixed with the displayed definition. The lab verifies the indexing directly on a transparent ordered sequence.

Use horizon repricing rather than treating risk-factor returns as portfolio returns. With options, time to expiry decreases, a volatility scenario may change implied volatility, and some contracts pay cash or mature. Keeping today's holdings fixed is a scenario-policy assumption; it is different from simulating an intrahorizon rebalancing strategy. A univariate factor proxy also leaves residual risks unless the portfolio is actually determined by that factor.

## 6. Gaussian VaR and its time units [20:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AG4GLF9SBM0&t=1219s)

For a small-horizon linear approximation to a long asset of current monetary size \(N\), suppose physical return is normal with mean \(\mu h\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\sqrt h\). Here \(h\) is years, and \(\sigma\) is annual volatility per square-root year. Then

\[
\operatorname{VaR}_\alpha\approx-N\mu h+N\sigma\sqrt h\,z_\alpha,
\qquad z_\alpha=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha).
\]

Risk forecasting uses physical drift, not an automatic replacement by the pricing rate \(r\). Dropping drift is a common short-horizon approximation when its monetary contribution is small relative to the tail term, not a theorem that expected return is zero. State whether years contain 252 trading days or another chosen time basis before converting daily inputs.

The source invests 100,000 dollars in an asset with annual volatility 30%, assumes 252 trading days, ignores drift, and asks for five-day 99% VaR. Full-precision \(z_{0.99}=2.3263478740\) gives 9830.61401862 dollars. Its rounded \(z=2.33\) gives 9846.04706759. The difference is rounding, not a distinct risk model. For two positions of 100,000 dollars each, daily volatility 1% each and correlation 0.3, daily monetary standard deviation is \(\sqrt{2{,}600{,}000}=1612.45154966\) dollars. Five-day 99% VaR is 8387.76654442 using the precise normal quantile.

The square-root rule is exact for centered Gaussian sums of independent identically distributed monetary increments with fixed exposures. With a nonzero constant drift, the mean scales with \(h\) while only the standard deviation scales with \(\sqrt h\). For stationary daily increments of variance \(s^2\) and autocorrelation \(\rho_k\),

\[
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^mG_j\Big)
=s^2\left[m+2\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}(m-k)\rho_k\right].
\]

The identity comes from counting each covariance at lag \(k\) exactly \(m-k\) times in the variance double sum. For \(\rho_k=0.4^k,m=5\), the bracket is 9.4672 rather than 5. Even Gaussian autocorrelated increments then defeat naive square-root scaling. Nonlinear portfolio payoffs and changing exposures create further failures. Under exact physical GBM, a long asset's loss quantile is \(N[1-e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)h-\sigma\sqrt h\,z_\alpha}]\); its loss is bounded above by \(N\), unlike the normal linear approximation.

## 7. Tail severity and probability atoms

For an integrable loss, expected shortfall averages the worst \(1-\alpha\) probability mass:

\[
\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(L)
=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_\alpha^1\operatorname{VaR}_u(L)\,du.
\]

When loss has no atom at its threshold and the tail is well defined, this agrees with the familiar conditional tail mean. With atoms it generally differs from both \(\mathbb E[L\mid L>v]\) and \(\mathbb E[L\mid L\ge v]\). For finite losses, include all mass strictly above \(v\), then only enough mass at \(v\) to fill the required tail probability. [Rockafellar–Uryasev's general-distribution paper, Definition 3 and Proposition 8](https://sites.math.washington.edu/~rtr/papers/rtr187-CVaR2.pdf) supports this distinction; their earlier [1999 paper, pages 4–5](https://sites.math.washington.edu/~rtr/papers/rtr179-CVaR1.pdf) explicitly restricts its initial tail-mean treatment to continuous CDFs.

Consider the original finite loss law

| Loss | Probability |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0.96 |
| 10 | 0.03 |
| 100 | 0.01 |

At 95%, VaR is zero. The worst 5% comprises the entire 1% mass at 100, the 3% mass at 10, and 1% of zero-loss states. Thus ES is \((0.01\cdot100+0.03\cdot10)/0.05=26\). Conditioning on \(L\ge0\) instead gives 1.3, while conditioning on \(L>0\) gives 32.5; neither is 26. At 97.5%, VaR is 10 and ES is \((0.01\cdot100+0.015\cdot10)/0.025=46\). Increasing the extreme loss from 100 to 1000 leaves 95% VaR unchanged at zero while raising ES to 206. The quantile alone cannot describe tail severity.

An independently useful way to compute ES is the convex objective

\[
\min_a\left\{a+\frac{\mathbb E[(L-a)^+]}{1-\alpha}\right\}.
\]

For a finite law, this formula can be proved directly. Write $\beta=1-\alpha$ and $J(a)=a+\mathbb E[(L-a)^+]/\beta$. Each hinge $(\ell-a)^+$ is convex in $a$, so $J$ is convex and piecewise linear. At a loss value, its left and right slopes are

$$J'_-(a)=1-\frac{\Pr(L\ge a)}{\beta}
=\frac{F_L(a-)-\alpha}{\beta},
\qquad
J'_+(a)=1-\frac{\Pr(L>a)}{\beta}
=\frac{F_L(a)-\alpha}{\beta}.$$

The left derivative counts the atom at $a$, while the right derivative excludes it. A convex function is minimized precisely when its left slope is nonpositive and its right slope is nonnegative. Hence the minimizers obey $F_L(a-)\le\alpha\le F_L(a)$. The lower VaR is one such point; it need not be the only one. In the displayed law at confidence 96%, every $a$ between 0 and 10 minimizes the objective because the intervening CDF is exactly 0.96.

For any minimizing threshold $a$, substitute into the objective:

$$\beta J(a)=\mathbb E[L\mathbf1_{\{L>a\}}]
+a[\beta-\Pr(L>a)].$$

The bracket is nonnegative and at most $\Pr(L=a)$. This is exactly all the loss mass strictly above the threshold plus the fraction of threshold mass required to fill probability $\beta$. Division by $\beta$ proves equality with the finite quantile-integral definition. It also explains why the threshold itself and the minimized risk value are different outputs of the optimization. The general result is [Rockafellar–Uryasev, Theorem 10](https://sites.math.washington.edu/~rtr/papers/rtr187-CVaR2.pdf). The lab computes ES both by sorted tail filling and by minimizing over finite loss breakpoints, providing separate algorithmic checks.

A second finite formulation explains the aggregation property rather than merely checking it on an example. On joint scenarios $j$ with probabilities $p_j$ and losses $\ell_j$, choose weights $w_j$ satisfying $0\le w_j\le p_j/\beta$ and $\sum_jw_j=1$. Then

$$\operatorname{ES}_\alpha(L)
=\max_w\sum_jw_j\ell_j.$$

To prove this, allocate worst-tail probability $\beta w_j$ to scenarios, never using more than their available mass $p_j$. Moving allocated mass from a lower loss to an unfilled higher loss cannot reduce the weighted average. Repeating this move fills all highest losses first and at most part of the threshold atom, exactly the preceding ES construction. Equivalently, for any threshold $a$ and feasible weights,

$$\sum_jw_j\ell_j
=a+\sum_jw_j(\ell_j-a)
\le a+\frac1\beta\sum_jp_j(\ell_j-a)^+=J(a).$$

The tail-filling weights attain equality at a minimizing threshold. Thus ES is the worst expected loss under bounded probability reweightings: no scenario can receive more than $1/\beta$ times its original probability. This is a mathematical risk envelope, not a claim that one of these reweighted laws is the true forecasting distribution.

For two losses defined on the same joint scenarios, the feasible weight set is the same for each. Therefore $\max_w\sum_jw_j(\ell_j^{(1)}+\ell_j^{(2)})$ is at most the sum of the two separate maxima. This proves finite-law ES subadditivity without an independence assumption. Dependence still affects the joint scenarios and the resulting numerical risk; it has not disappeared from the model.

VaR need not reward aggregation. Let two independent positions each lose 100 with probability 0.04 and zero otherwise. Their individual 95% VaRs are zero. The sum has zero-loss probability \(0.96^2=0.9216\), so its 95% VaR is 100: larger than the sum of the individual VaRs. In this construction each ES is 80, while combined ES is 103.2, below 160. The example is an exact finite counterexample to VaR subadditivity; it does not imply that every diversification reduces every portfolio's risk.
