16. From random walks to Brownian risk factors

Recording 37, Recording 38, Recording 39 · Expanded, independently developed exposition

A risk desk records a market factor once a day and wants to estimate its uncertainty over a quarter. Doubling the number of observations should not double the uncertainty of the same quarter. Yet a model that adds an independent unit-sized shock at every observation does exactly that. Refining the clock changes the model unless the shock size changes with it.

We use one deliberately small model throughout: a dimensionless cumulative factor $Y_t$ with an annual drift $\mu$ and volatility scale $\sigma$. It could represent an additive approximation to a return or a standardized market signal. It is not yet a positive stock-price model, and its probability law is a physical scenario model rather than a pricing rule. The task is to design a time refinement that preserves its intended horizon mean and variance, then determine exactly what that construction establishes.

1. A process needs joint probabilities, not only a histogram 08:14

A random variable assigns a numerical outcome to each modeled state. A stochastic process is a family of random variables, indexed here by time and defined on the same probability space. Writing $Y_t$ for every time $t$ specifies a process only when the relationships among those variables are also specified. A sample path is the time-indexed sequence obtained in one realization of all the underlying randomness.

A deterministic evolution rule and its exact initial state instead determine the modeled future without additional random draws. A deterministic system can nevertheless be difficult to forecast when initial measurements are imprecise or its dynamics amplify small errors. Chaotic sensitivity is one such source of forecast difficulty; it does not by itself turn a deterministic rule into a stochastic law. A stochastic market model is a chosen probabilistic description of uncertainty, not a conclusion that follows merely from unsuccessful prediction.

The distinction is visible without advanced mathematics. Let a fair sign $Z$ take values $-1$ and $1$, each with probability $1/2$. In model A, set $Y_k=Z$ at every integer time $k\ge1$. In model B, draw a fresh independent fair sign at each time and use it for $Y_k$. At each individual time, both models have exactly the same distribution. But model A has $\Pr(Y_2=Y_1)=1$, while model B has probability $1/2$. A two-day exposure therefore depends on information that a one-day histogram cannot supply.

The joint distribution assigns probabilities to simultaneous statements about several times. If $y_0,\ldots,y_n$ are possible values, the conditional-probability chain rule gives

$$\Pr(Y_0=y_0,\ldots,Y_n=y_n) =\Pr(Y_0=y_0)\prod_{j=1}^{n} \Pr(Y_j=y_j\mid Y_0=y_0,\ldots,Y_{j-1}=y_{j-1}).$$

Here $\Pr$ denotes probability under the scenario model. The formula follows by repeatedly applying $\Pr(A\cap B)=\Pr(B\mid A)\Pr(A)$; when a conditioning event has zero probability, an appropriate conditional-law formulation is needed instead of a ratio of probabilities. Continuous-state processes use probability kernels or densities where available rather than point probabilities.

The Markov property says that, conditional on the current state and time, the conditional distribution of the future does not require the earlier history. In the discrete notation above it replaces each long conditioning list by $Y_{j-1}=y_{j-1}$. It concerns a conditional distribution, not an assertion that the next outcome is determined without randomness. It also depends on which variables are included in the state.

For example, suppose a walker has direction $D_k\in\{-1,1\}$ and position $X_k$. It keeps its direction on the next step with probability $a=3/4$ and reverses it with probability $1/4$, then updates $X_{k+1}=X_k+D_{k+1}$. The two histories $(X_0,X_1,X_2)=(0,1,0)$ and $(0,-1,0)$ end at the same position but have opposite last directions. The next up-step probabilities are respectively $1/4$ and $3/4$. Position alone is not a sufficient Markov state. The pair $(X_k,D_k)$ is sufficient under the stated transition rule.

This is a practical modeling distinction. Calling a sampled price series Markov does not establish that omitted volatility, regime, order-flow, or other state variables are irrelevant. Adding a useful state variable can simplify the transition rule while increasing the state dimension. Whether that enlarged model fits data is an empirical question separate from the definition.

2. Time discretization and state discretization are separate choices 20:09

A discrete-time model specifies random variables only on an indexed time grid. A continuous-time model specifies them throughout an interval. Either kind can have a discrete or continuous set of possible state values. Observing a continuous process at daily closes produces a discrete-time observation sequence; it does not prove that the unobserved market cannot move between closes.

Fix a horizon $T>0$ and an integer number of steps $n$. Let $\Delta t=T/n$ and $t_j=j\Delta t$, for $j=0,\ldots,n$. These definitions separate the physical time length from the number of grid intervals. Refining the grid means increasing $n$ while keeping $T$ fixed.

Let $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$ be independent fair signs, each equal to $1$ or $-1$ with probability $1/2$. The unscaled walk $U_j=\sum_{i=1}^j\xi_i$ starts at $U_0=0$. Each shock has expectation zero and variance one. Independence implies that distinct shocks have zero covariance, so

$$\mathbb E[U_j]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(U_j)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\operatorname{Var}(\xi_i)=j.$$

The symbol $\mathbb E$ denotes expectation and $\operatorname{Var}(Z)=\mathbb E[Z^2]-(\mathbb E[Z])^2$ denotes variance. The equality of variance to the sum of individual variances uses zero covariance; it would not follow from identical marginal distributions alone. The persistent-direction example in section 1 deliberately violates independent increments.

At the fixed horizon $T$, the unscaled variance is $n$. It diverges when we insert more steps. This does not reveal an economic law that observing more frequently creates volatility. It reveals that a unit shock per increasingly short interval is an inconsistent family of models for the intended fixed-horizon risk.

For two steps, the endpoints are $-2,0,2$ with probabilities $1/4,1/2,1/4$. The middle endpoint has two contributing paths. Its probability cannot be obtained by assigning equal weight to three endpoint labels. Path counting and state counting are different operations, and that distinction will also matter in a binomial pricing tree.

3. Square-root scaling preserves the variance rate 07:06

Define the scaled factor on the grid by

$$Y^{(n)}_{t_j}=\mu t_j+ \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\sum_{i=1}^{j}\xi_i.$$

The superscript $(n)$ identifies the discretization. The drift $\mu$ has units of factor per year and $\sigma$ has units of factor per square-root year. Thus each random increment has the same factor units as $Y$. A time increment $\Delta t$ changes the mean by $\mu\Delta t$ and contributes variance $\sigma^2\Delta t$.

The square root follows from a variance calculation. Multiplying a random variable by a constant $b$ multiplies its variance by $b^2$. Therefore a shock of magnitude $\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$ has variance $\sigma^2\Delta t$. Summing $j$ independent increments gives

$$\mathbb E[Y^{(n)}_{t_j}]=\mu t_j, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y^{(n)}_{t_j})=\sigma^2t_j.$$

At $T$, both expressions are independent of the chosen step count. Scaling shocks proportionally to $\Delta t$ instead would produce horizon variance $n\sigma^2(\Delta t)^2=\sigma^2T^2/n$, which tends to zero. Keeping their size fixed would make the variance diverge. Square-root scaling is the nondegenerate choice for this independent finite-variance construction.

Take $T=1$, $n=4$, $\mu=0.04$, and $\sigma=0.2$. Each interval has drift $0.01$ and random shock $\pm0.1$. The endpoint law, calculated from the number of positive signs, is

Number of positive signs Endpoint factor Probability
0 -0.36 1/16
1 -0.16 4/16
2 0.04 6/16
3 0.24 4/16
4 0.44 1/16

Its mean is $0.04$ and variance is $0.04$. These are model quantities, not claims about an observed security. Matching two moments does not establish the normal distribution or the joint path law. The table itself is discrete and has bounded support, whereas the limiting normal law has a density on the whole real line.

The table can be checked without invoking the general moment formula. Relative to the mean, the endpoint deviations are $-0.4,-0.2,0,0.2,0.4$. Symmetric weights make the weighted deviations cancel. The weighted squared deviations sum to $[0.16+4(0.04)+4(0.04)+0.16]/16=0.04$. The zero middle deviation has weight 6/16 and contributes nothing to variance. This calculation checks both the scaling and the combinatorial probabilities.

At a time that is not a grid point, a piecewise-constant version uses $j=\lfloor t/\Delta t\rfloor$ completed shocks. The floor symbol means the largest integer not exceeding its argument. Its random variance is $\sigma^2\lfloor t/\Delta t\rfloor\Delta t$, not exactly $\sigma^2t$. The difference is smaller than $\sigma^2\Delta t$ and vanishes as the grid is refined. Exact grid identities and limiting identities should not be conflated.

4. A normal endpoint limit is not a complete process construction 18:07

For a fixed positive $T$, the independent-sign central limit theorem gives $n^{-1/2}\sum_i\xi_i$ converging in distribution to a standard normal variable. Convergence in distribution means convergence of probabilities of threshold events at continuity points of the limiting distribution; it does not mean that each realized path settles to a particular curve.

The limit can be inspected directly using a characteristic function, the expectation $\mathbb E[e^{ikZ}]$ of a random variable $Z$, where $i^2=-1$ and $k$ is a real frequency. For a fair sign, $\mathbb E[e^{ikb\xi}]=(e^{ikb}+e^{-ikb})/2=\cos(kb)$. Independence turns the characteristic function of a sum into a product. Hence

$$\mathbb E[e^{ikY_T^{(n)}}] =e^{ik\mu T}\left[\cos\left(k\sigma\sqrt{T/n}\right)\right]^n.$$

For a small real $z$, $\log\cos z=-z^2/2+O(z^4)$. The notation $O(z^4)$ denotes a remainder bounded by a constant times $|z|^4$ near zero. Multiplication by $n$ makes the leading term $-k^2\sigma^2T/2$ and the remainder of order $1/n$. Thus the limit characteristic function is $e^{ik\mu T-k^2\sigma^2T/2}$, that of a normal variable with mean $\mu T$ and variance $\sigma^2T$.

Using the standard continuity theorem for characteristic functions identifies the endpoint distribution. The theorem itself is cited background probability theory rather than proved by the short expansion. The calculation exposes why the drift and variance survive refinement and why higher terms disappear in this particular independent bounded-increment model.

A whole process requires more. Independent increments over disjoint blocks allow a corresponding joint-distribution argument at finitely many times. To obtain convergence of interpolated paths in an appropriate function space, one also needs a tightness argument controlling oscillations between observation times. That functional convergence theorem is not proved here. We instead give the Brownian process definition explicitly and use its stated properties. Kogan's MIT notes, slides 4–5 present the scaled-walk motivation and independent normal increments.

5. Brownian motion specifies both increments and path regularity 24:39

A standard Brownian motion $W_t$, for $t\ge0$, starts at $W_0=0$, has continuous sample paths with probability one, and has independent normal increments: for $0\le s<t$, $W_t-W_s$ is normal with mean zero and variance $t-s$, independently of the history up to $s$. Independent increments concern disjoint future shocks; the levels themselves are not independent.

For $s\le t$, write $W_t=W_s+(W_t-W_s)$. The second term is independent of $W_s$ and has mean zero, so $\mathbb E[W_sW_t]=\mathbb E[W_s^2]=s$. Consequently

$$\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t).$$

Here $\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)=\mathbb E[(A-\mathbb EA)(B-\mathbb EB)]$. The covariance grows with the shared portion of the history. For example, two factor observations at half a year and one year have covariance $\sigma^2/2$ under the drifted model. Treating the observations as independent would discard that common accumulated shock.

The continuous factor model is now

$$Y_t=\mu t+\sigma W_t.$$

It has mean $\mu t$, variance $\sigma^2t$, and future increment $Y_{t+h}-Y_t$ normal with mean $\mu h$ and variance $\sigma^2h$. This gives a precise transition law. It does not establish that actual market increments are independent, normal, or time homogeneous. Those are testable modeling assumptions.

Brownian paths are almost surely nowhere differentiable, a path-regularity theorem quoted here, not inferred from a jagged plot. A finite linearly interpolated walk is differentiable between its finitely many corner points. Its picture therefore cannot prove the Brownian theorem. The scale calculation helps explain the obstruction: a typical increment is of order $\sqrt h$, so dividing it by $h$ produces a magnitude of order $1/\sqrt h$, rather than the finite local slope expected for a differentiable path. This is intuition about scaling, not the full almost-sure proof.

Likewise, $\Delta W\sim\mathcal N(0,\Delta t)$ is a statement about a finite increment's distribution. Writing $\Delta W=\sqrt{\Delta t}Z$ is valid with $Z$ a standard normal variable for that increment. It is not a license to use one independent $Z$ for all overlapping time intervals. Their dependence must remain consistent with the covariance just derived.

The distinction affects a multi-date risk calculation. Suppose a desk pays the factor observed at half a year plus the factor observed at one year. Its variance is the sum of the individual variances plus twice their covariance: $\sigma^2(1/2)+\sigma^2(1)+2\sigma^2(1/2)=2.5\sigma^2$. Treating the two observations as independent would give only $1.5\sigma^2$. At $\sigma=0.2$, these are 0.10 and 0.06. The observation dates have not changed; the difference is the omitted shared history. This is why a path model matters even before an option payoff is introduced.

6. A lattice transition rule leads to a diffusion equation 18:34

Let $m_j(t)$ be the probability mass at the lattice point $x_j=j\Delta x$. If a symmetric walker moves left or right with probability $1/2$ each over time $\Delta t$, conditioning on its previous position gives the exact recursion

$$m_j(t+\Delta t)=\tfrac12m_{j-1}(t)+\tfrac12m_{j+1}(t).$$

Probability arriving at $j$ comes from its two neighbors. Summing the right-hand side over all $j$ preserves total mass, because each old mass contributes halves to two destinations. The recursion is an exact finite-grid probability statement before any derivative is taken.

Suppose a smooth density approximation exists, with $m_j(t)\approx\rho(x_j,t)\Delta x$. A density $\rho$ gives probability by integration over an interval; its value at a point is not itself a point probability. Taylor expansion at $(x_j,t)$ gives

$$\Delta t\,\partial_t\rho =\tfrac12(\Delta x)^2\partial_{xx}\rho +\text{higher-order terms}.$$

If $(\Delta x)^2/\Delta t\to\sigma^2$ while the higher-order terms vanish in the scaled limit, the candidate density equation is $\partial_t\rho=(\sigma^2/2)\partial_{xx}\rho$. The coefficient is a variance rate divided by two. Dropping the square on $\Delta x$ or the factor $1/2$ changes that rate.

Adding drift produces the constant-coefficient forward diffusion equation

$$\partial_t\rho=-\mu\partial_x\rho+ \tfrac12\sigma^2\partial_{xx}\rho.$$

One way to see the drift sign is to translate a density rightward with speed $\mu$: $\rho(x,t)=g(x-\mu t)$ satisfies $\partial_t\rho=-\mu\partial_x\rho$. Diffusion spreads probability around that moving center. The equation evolves a probability distribution, not a sample path.

Starting the process exactly at zero means an initial point mass, denoted $\delta_0$. It is not a finite ordinary density equal to one at zero and zero elsewhere, which would integrate to zero. The initial condition is interpreted through test functions: $\int f(x)\rho(x,t)\,dx\to f(0)$ as $t\downarrow0$ for appropriate bounded continuous $f$. The next chapter solves the heat equation by Fourier transformation and connects this density calculation to stochastic calculus.

This Taylor argument is a consistency derivation under smoothness and limit assumptions. By itself it does not prove existence of the limiting process, convergence of every lattice solution, or uniqueness under arbitrary boundary conditions. On a bounded domain, boundary behavior such as absorption or reflection must be specified as additional model data.

7. Executable checks and their boundary

The chapter laboratory computes the exact binomial endpoint law by counting independent-sign paths. For step counts 1,4,16,64,256 it checks total probability, mean 0.04, and variance 0.04. It independently enumerates all four-step paths to check a covariance of 0.02 between half-year and one-year factor observations. These checks compare distinct representations of the same finite construction.

The lab compares the exact characteristic function with its normal limit at frequency 2. With zero drift, the four-step value is 0.92261884 and the normal value is 0.92311635. The 256-step value is 0.92310865. A continuity-corrected cumulative-distribution comparison gives maximum errors 0.00620967 for four steps and 0.00042577 for 64 steps at the chosen lattice boundaries. These are computed diagnostics for this family, not a general convergence-rate theorem.

Inputs require a positive integer step count, positive finite horizon, finite drift, and nonnegative finite volatility. The program does not estimate these parameters from data, generate orders, or certify a market model. It also prints the two different next-step probabilities 0.25 and 0.75 for the persistent-direction example. A regression that ignored direction would be fitting a different transition law.

Exercises

  1. For $n$ independent fair signs, derive the endpoint distribution of their sum and verify its mean and variance without a simulation.
  2. Let independent increments have drift $\mu\Delta t$ and symmetric shock magnitude $b(\Delta t)$. Determine which scaling of $b$ gives a finite nonzero variance rate. Explain what happens with fixed-size and time-proportional shocks.
  3. Derive the covariance of the scaled factor at two grid times $s\le t$. Which independence statement is valid, and which is not?
  4. For the persistent-direction walker with retention probability 3/4, verify the two conditional next-up probabilities at position zero after two steps. Give a sufficient enlarged Markov state.
  5. Derive the exact symmetric-lattice mass recursion. Show preservation of mass and derive the candidate diffusion coefficient by Taylor expansion.
  6. A risk analyst claims that normally distributed observations at each time prove the entire model is Brownian motion. Give a counterexample and list the additional Brownian requirements the analyst omitted.
Fully worked solutions

  1. If exactly $k$ of the $n$ signs are positive, the sum is $k-(n-k)=2k-n$. There are $\binom nk$ such paths, each with probability $2^{-n}$, so $\Pr(U_n=2k-n)=\binom nk2^{-n}$. Summing independent zero-mean signs gives expectation zero. Since each square equals one, each variance is one, and all pair covariances vanish by independence. Thus $\operatorname{Var}(U_n)=n$. In the two-step case the probabilities are 1/4,1/2,1/4, not three equal probabilities.

  2. There are $T/\Delta t$ increments over a grid-aligned horizon $T$. Their mean sum is $\mu T$. Their variance sum is $(T/\Delta t)b(\Delta t)^2$. A nonzero finite variance rate $\sigma^2$ requires $b(\Delta t)^2/\Delta t\to\sigma^2$, hence $b$ of order $\sqrt{\Delta t}$. A fixed positive magnitude makes the variance diverge. Taking $b=\sigma\Delta t$ makes it tend to zero. The square-root rule is tied to independent finite-variance increments; other dependence or tail assumptions can produce other limits.

  3. Write the centered factor at $t$ as its centered value at $s$ plus the sum of increments after $s$. The later sum is independent of the earlier sum and has mean zero. Their covariance is therefore the variance accumulated up to $s$, namely $\sigma^2s$. Reversing the time order yields $\sigma^2\min(s,t)$. Disjoint increments are independent, but the two levels share the early increments and are not independent when $s>0$ and $\sigma>0$.

  4. The path $0,1,0$ ends with direction $-1$, so an up-step requires reversal and has probability $1/4$. The path $0,-1,0$ ends with direction $+1$, so an up-step requires retention and has probability $3/4$. Both histories have positive probability under a fair initial direction and the stated transition rule. Their present positions coincide while their future laws differ. The pair $(X_k,D_k)$ records both the current position and last direction, making the next conditional law depend only on the present pair.

  5. To arrive at point $j$ at the next time, the previous walker must be at $j-1$ and move right or at $j+1$ and move left. The disjoint events give $m_j(t+\Delta t)=[m_{j-1}(t)+m_{j+1}(t)]/2$. Summing over all $j$ counts each old mass twice with coefficient 1/2, preserving total mass. Replacing $m_j$ by $\rho(x_j,t)\Delta x$ and expanding yields $\Delta t\partial_t\rho=(\Delta x)^2\partial_{xx}\rho/2$ to leading order. The variance-rate limit $(\Delta x)^2/\Delta t\to\sigma^2$ gives diffusion coefficient $\sigma^2/2$.

  6. Let $Z$ be one standard normal variable and define $X_t=\sqrt t Z$ for $t\ge0$. Every $X_t$ is normal with mean zero and variance $t$, and the paths are continuous. But $\operatorname{Cov}(X_s,X_t)=\sqrt{st}$ rather than $\min(s,t)$, and future increments are not independent of the past. For $s>0$, observing $X_s$ determines $Z$ and hence the entire future in this construction. Brownian motion additionally requires independent normal increments of the specified interval variance and almost-sure path continuity. Marginal normality alone does not supply them.

"""Check scaled-walk laws and a hidden-memory example; no network or files."""
from math import comb, cos, erf, exp, isclose, isfinite, sqrt


def walk_law(steps, horizon=1.0, drift=0.04, volatility=0.2):
    """Return the exact binomial endpoint law on its finite support."""
    if not isinstance(steps, int) or steps <= 0:
        raise ValueError("steps must be a positive integer")
    if not all(isfinite(x) for x in [horizon, drift, volatility]):
        raise ValueError("parameters must be finite")
    if horizon <= 0 or volatility < 0:
        raise ValueError("invalid horizon or volatility")
    jump = volatility * sqrt(horizon / steps)
    return [(drift * horizon + (2 * k - steps) * jump,
             comb(steps, k) / 2 ** steps) for k in range(steps + 1)]


def normal_cdf(value):
    return (1 + erf(value / sqrt(2))) / 2


def corrected_error(law, mean, standard_deviation):
    """Largest CDF error at continuity-corrected lattice boundaries."""
    if len(law) < 2 or standard_deviation <= 0:
        raise ValueError("need a nondegenerate lattice")
    spacing = law[1][0] - law[0][0]
    cumulative = 0.0
    errors = []
    for value, probability in law:
        cumulative += probability
        boundary = value + spacing / 2
        approx = normal_cdf((boundary - mean) / standard_deviation)
        errors.append(abs(cumulative - approx))
    return max(errors)


def main():
    four = walk_law(4)
    expected_values = [-0.36, -0.16, 0.04, 0.24, 0.44]
    expected_probabilities = [1 / 16, 4 / 16, 6 / 16, 4 / 16, 1 / 16]
    for (value, probability), target, chance in zip(
            four, expected_values, expected_probabilities):
        assert isclose(value, target, abs_tol=1e-14)
        assert probability == chance
    print("four-step endpoint law:",
          [(round(x, 4), p) for x, p in four])
    for steps in [1, 4, 16, 64, 256]:
        law = walk_law(steps)
        mean = sum(x * p for x, p in law)
        variance = sum((x - mean) ** 2 * p for x, p in law)
        assert isclose(sum(p for _, p in law), 1, abs_tol=1e-13)
        assert isclose(mean, 0.04, abs_tol=1e-13)
        assert isclose(variance, 0.04, abs_tol=1e-13)
    # Covariance of two nested walk sums equals the shared increment variance.
    dt, sigma = 0.25, 0.2
    paths = [(a, b, c, d) for a in [-1, 1] for b in [-1, 1]
             for c in [-1, 1] for d in [-1, 1]]
    covariance = sum((sigma * sqrt(dt) * (a + b))
                     * (sigma * sqrt(dt) * (a + b + c + d))
                     for a, b, c, d in paths) / len(paths)
    assert isclose(covariance, sigma ** 2 * 0.5, abs_tol=1e-14)
    two_date_variance = sum(
        (sigma * sqrt(dt) * (2 * a + 2 * b + c + d)) ** 2
        for a, b, c, d in paths) / len(paths)
    assert isclose(two_date_variance, 0.10, abs_tol=1e-14)
    assert isclose(sigma ** 2 * 1.5, 0.06, abs_tol=1e-14)
    # Endpoint characteristic function, checked against finite enumeration.
    frequency = 2.0
    for steps in [4, 64, 256]:
        law = walk_law(steps, drift=0)
        enumerated = sum(cos(frequency * x) * p for x, p in law)
        product = cos(frequency * 0.2 / sqrt(steps)) ** steps
        assert isclose(enumerated, product, abs_tol=1e-13)
        print(f"n={steps}: characteristic={product:.8f}, "
              f"normal={exp(-0.5 * (frequency * 0.2) ** 2):.8f}")
    errors = [corrected_error(walk_law(n), 0.04, 0.2) for n in [4, 64]]
    assert errors[1] < errors[0]
    print("continuity-corrected CDF errors:",
          [round(x, 8) for x in errors])
    # A persistent direction makes position alone non-Markov.
    retention = 0.75
    p_up_after_up_down = 1 - retention
    p_up_after_down_up = retention
    assert p_up_after_up_down != p_up_after_down_up
    assert sum([1 / 2, 1 / 2]) == 1
    print("next-up probabilities at the same position:",
          p_up_after_up_down, p_up_after_down_up)
    for steps in [0, -1, 2.5]:
        try:
            walk_law(steps)
        except ValueError:
            pass
        else:
            raise AssertionError("invalid step count accepted")
    print("normalization, moments, covariance and finite-law checks passed")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
