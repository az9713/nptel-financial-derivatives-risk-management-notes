A desk uses a normal return approximation to estimate how much cash it needs for the next year. Another analyst models prices as positive exponentials of a Brownian factor and obtains a different tail probability. A third asks whether the cash buffer must remain positive throughout the year, rather than only on the last day. These are different mathematical questions. Changing a formula without identifying the random variable or event can produce a precise answer to the wrong question.

We retain the Brownian driver $W_t$ from chapter 16 and use it to build a strictly positive price $S_t$. Before interpreting that model, we need a calculus that respects the accumulated squared increments of Brownian motion. The correction is not a convention added to repair an inconvenient expectation. It follows from a limit of finite sums whose algebra can be inspected directly.

1. The heat kernel solves a distribution problem 00:39

For standard Brownian motion starting at zero, the candidate density $\rho(x,t)$ satisfies $\partial_t\rho=\tfrac12\partial_{xx}\rho$ for $t>0$, with a unit point mass at time zero. Here $x$ is a possible realized value of $W_t$, while $t$ is elapsed time. The equation describes the distribution over possible values, not the derivative of one realized Brownian path.

Define the Fourier transform $\widehat\rho(k,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(x,t)e^{-ikx}\,dx$, where $k$ is a real frequency and $i^2=-1$. Under integrability and sufficient decay to justify differentiating and integrating by parts, the second spatial derivative transforms to $-k^2\widehat\rho$. The density equation becomes the ordinary differential equation

$$\partial_t\widehat\rho=-\tfrac12k^2\widehat\rho.$$

The point-mass initial condition has transform one. Solving the equation therefore gives $\widehat\rho(k,t)=e^{-k^2t/2}$. Inverting the transform yields

$$\rho(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2t}\right),\qquad t>0.$$

The inverse integral is Gaussian. Its normalization is fixed by $\int e^{-tk^2/2}dk=\sqrt{2\pi/t}$. Its dependence on $x$ can also be verified without a complex change of contour: differentiate the inverse integral with respect to $x$ and integrate once by parts in $k$ to obtain $\partial_x\rho=-(x/t)\rho$. The solution has the displayed exponential form, and normalization determines the prefactor.

The result integrates to one, has mean zero, and has variance $t$. It supplies the normal transition density with the correct clock scaling. It does not replace the independent-increment and continuous-path requirements in the Brownian definition. The same one-time density can belong to a different process, as the counterexample in chapter 16 showed.

2. The central limit theorem needs its hypotheses 05:45

For independent identically distributed variables $X_i$ with finite mean $m$ and finite strictly positive variance $v$, the classical central limit theorem states that

$$Z_n=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i-nm}{\sqrt{nv}} \ \Longrightarrow\ \mathcal N(0,1).$$

The arrow denotes convergence in distribution, and $\mathcal N(0,1)$ is the normal distribution with mean zero and variance one. The conclusion concerns a standardized sum, not an assertion that each individual $X_i$ becomes normal. The theorem does not require the individual distribution to be Gaussian, but it does require more than a vague assertion that many small effects are present.

For example, take $X_i$ uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$. Integration gives $m=\int_0^1x\,dx=1/2$ and $v=\int_0^1x^2\,dx-m^2=1/12$. Centering and rescaling one such variable gives $U_i=\sqrt{12}(X_i-1/2)$. Its characteristic function is $\sin(\sqrt3 k)/(\sqrt3 k)$, interpreted as one at $k=0$. The characteristic function of $n^{-1/2}\sum_iU_i$ is the $n$th power of its rescaled value. Expanding $\sin z/z=1-z^2/6+O(z^4)$ produces the limit $e^{-k^2/2}$.

This calculation explains the source example's normal limit without assuming that uniform and normal observations are interchangeable at finite $n$. A finite standardized uniform sum has bounded support. A normal variable does not. Dependence, a changing distribution across observations, or an infinite second moment requires a different theorem and may produce a different limit.

The square-root clock in chapter 16 is one consequence of the independent finite-variance setting. It is not proof that market tails have finite variance, that observed returns are independent, or that a one-day normal approximation can be extrapolated to any horizon without checking its assumptions.

3. Squared increments accumulate instead of disappearing 04:00

Partition $[0,T]$ into $n$ equal intervals of length $h=T/n$, and let $\Delta W_j=W_{jh}-W_{(j-1)h}$. Brownian increments are independent normals with mean zero and variance $h$. Thus $\Delta W_j=\sqrt h Z_j$ in distribution, with independent standard normal $Z_j$.

The quadratic-variation sum is $Q_n=\sum_j(\Delta W_j)^2$. Since $\mathbb E[Z^2]=1$ and $\mathbb E[Z^4]=3$, its mean and variance are

$$\mathbb E[Q_n]=nh=T,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Q_n)=2nh^2=\frac{2T^2}{n}.$$

The fourth moment follows by integration by parts twice in the Gaussian density: $\mathbb E[Z^{2m}]=(2m-1)\mathbb E[Z^{2m-2}]$, so the second moment is one and the fourth is three. The boundary terms vanish because a Gaussian exponential decays faster than the polynomial factor. Subtracting the squared mean gives $\operatorname{Var}(Z^2)=3-1=2$.

Consequently $\mathbb E[(Q_n-T)^2]\to0$. This is mean-square convergence, which also implies convergence in probability. It justifies the limit $[W]_T=T$, where square brackets denote quadratic variation. General deterministic partitions with vanishing largest interval have the same result because their variance is $2\sum_j(\Delta t_j)^2\le2T\max_j\Delta t_j$.

A single finite squared increment is not deterministic. Its variance is $2h^2$ and its mean is $h$, so its standard deviation divided by its mean remains $\sqrt2$ as $h$ shrinks. The deterministic limit arises from the sum over many increments, not from declaring each $Z_j^2$ equal to one. The differential shorthand $(dW_t)^2=dt$ records this accumulated-limit rule. Kogan, slides 10–13 connects quadratic variation to the Itô formula.

The mean-square calculation also gives an explicit probability bound. For a positive tolerance $\varepsilon$, the elementary inequality $\Pr(Z^2\ge\varepsilon^2)\le\mathbb E[Z^2]/\varepsilon^2$ applies to $Z=Q_n-T$. The squared variable is at least $\varepsilon^2$ on the event being counted, which proves the inequality. It yields

$$\Pr(|Q_n-T|\ge\varepsilon)\le\frac{2T^2}{n\varepsilon^2}.$$

For $T=1$, $n=4096$, and $\varepsilon=0.1$, the bound is approximately 0.04882813. The observed seeded sum in the lab is closer to one, but an observed example is different from a probability guarantee. The bound is conservative and specific to independent Gaussian increments. Applying it unchanged to noisy transaction prices would require a measurement and dependence model.

4. The Itô integral explains the missing term 22:41

A process $H_t$ is adapted if its value at time $t$ is determined by information available then. For sufficiently regular adapted integrands, a left-point sum has the form $\sum_jH_{t_j}(W_{t_{j+1}}-W_{t_j})$. Let $\Omega$ denote the underlying space of random outcomes. If the sampled step integrands $H^{(n)}$ satisfy $\mathbb E\int_0^T|H_t^{(n)}-H_t|^2dt\to0$, this is convergence in the square-integrable space $L^2(\Omega\times[0,T])$, and these sums converge in mean square to the Itô integral $\int_0^TH_t\,dW_t$. Continuous adapted sample paths with a suitable square-integrable supremum give one sufficient setting for this construction. The Brownian integrand $H_t=W_t$ below has the needed approximation property.

For a general predictable integrand satisfying $\mathbb E\int_0^TH_t^2dt<\infty$, the integral is constructed by approximating it in that space with adapted step processes; arbitrary point-sampling need not converge. Predictable here means measurable with respect to the sigma-algebra generated by adapted left-continuous processes. For example, the deterministic integrand $H_t=\mathbf1_{\mathbb Q}(t)$, where $\mathbb Q$ here denotes the rational numbers, is zero almost everywhere in time and has Itô integral zero, while sampling rational mesh points would incorrectly produce $W_T$. Time-integrated equivalence, rather than isolated sampled values, determines the general integral.

Why does approximation in this particular norm produce a well-defined integral? Let $\mathcal F_t$ denote the information generated by the Brownian history up to time $t$. Begin with an adapted step process: on $[t_j,t_{j+1})$, let its value be $H_j$, known at $t_j$ and square-integrable. Its integral is the finite sum $I=\sum_jH_j\Delta W_j$. Conditional on $\mathcal F_{t_j}$, the next increment has mean zero and second moment $t_{j+1}-t_j$. For $i<j$, the earlier product $H_i\Delta W_i$ and the coefficient $H_j$ are already known at $t_j$. Conditioning there makes the cross term's expectation zero. Consequently

$$\mathbb E[I^2] =\sum_j\mathbb E[H_j^2](t_{j+1}-t_j) =\mathbb E\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt.$$

This identity is the Itô isometry: the mean squared size of the integral equals the time-integrated mean squared size of its integrand. Apply it to the difference of two step approximations. If their integrands approach the same $H$ in the stated norm, their integrals form a mean-square Cauchy sequence. Completeness of the square-integrable random variables gives a limit, and the same identity shows that different approximations to $H$ give the same limit. This is the continuity argument behind the construction, not an appeal to the visual smoothness of sampled paths.

For a two-interval check, let each interval have length $h=1/2$, take $H_0=1$, and take $H_1=1+W_h$. Then $I=W_h+(1+W_h)(W_1-W_h)$. The independent centered increments make its mean zero and its second moment $h+h(1+h)=1.25$, exactly the integrated second moment of $H$. The lab checks the equality by two-dimensional Gaussian quadrature. Random integrands are allowed; what matters is when their randomness becomes available.

In a valid adapted-step approximation, the integrand is evaluated before the next increment is observed. Conditional on the current information, the future Brownian increment has mean zero. The zero-mean step-integral property passes to the mean-square limit, so the integral has expectation zero under the stated integrability condition. Conditional expectations pass to that limit as well: the accumulated integral is a square-integrable martingale. It does not make its realized value zero. The same nonanticipating convention corresponds to deciding a trading position before its next price change.

For $H_t=W_t$, an exact finite algebraic identity exposes the correction:

$$W_T^2-W_0^2 =2\sum_jW_{t_j}\Delta W_j+\sum_j(\Delta W_j)^2.$$

This follows by expanding each $(W_{t_j}+\Delta W_j)^2-W_{t_j}^2$ and summing. Since $W_0=0$, the quadratic-variation limit gives

$$\int_0^TW_t\,dW_t=\frac{W_T^2-T}{2}.$$

The integral has mean zero because $\mathbb E[W_T^2]=T$, but it is a nonconstant random variable with variance $T^2/2$. It is bounded below by $-T/2$ and is therefore not normally distributed. Normal driving increments do not make every stochastic integral normal when the integrand is itself random.

More generally, suppose $X_t$ satisfies $dX_t=a(t,X_t)dt+b(t,X_t)dW_t$ in the Itô integral sense. For a function $f(t,x)$ with one continuous time derivative and two continuous spatial derivatives, and with the local integrability needed for the displayed integrals, the Itô formula is

$$df(t,X_t)=\left(f_t+a f_x+\tfrac12b^2f_{xx}\right)dt +b f_x\,dW_t.$$

Subscripts on $f$ denote partial derivatives, and $a,b$ are evaluated at $(t,X_t)$. A Taylor expansion motivates the formula: the spatial square term survives through quadratic variation, while products involving $dt^2$ and $dt\,dW_t$ disappear in the summed limit. A complete theorem requires control of the remainders; the polynomial argument above proves the square example exactly, while the general formula is an invoked standard result. MIT's Itô lecture, Theorem 1.1 and Example 1.3 develops these cases.

An exponential example will later connect the calculus to probability changes. Let $\lambda$ be a fixed real constant and set $M_t=\exp(\lambda W_t-\lambda^2t/2)$. Its partial time derivative contributes $-\lambda^2M_t/2$, while half its second spatial derivative contributes $+\lambda^2M_t/2$. The drift terms cancel and Itô's formula gives $dM_t=\lambda M_t\,dW_t$.

Let $\mathcal F_s$ denote the information generated by the Brownian history up to $s$. A process is a martingale when it is integrable and $\mathbb E[M_t\mid\mathcal F_s]=M_s$ for $s\le t$: conditional on present information, its expected future level is its present level. For this exponential, divide $M_t$ by $M_s$ and use the independent normal increment $W_t-W_s$. Its exponential expectation is $e^{\lambda^2(t-s)/2}$, which cancels the deterministic normalizing factor. Thus the conditional expected ratio is one.

The direct expectation check matters. In more general equations, zero formal drift alone does not establish all integrability requirements for a true martingale. The constant-coefficient Gaussian calculation verifies them here. This normalized exponential is a building block, not yet a claim that a chosen physical drift should be removed from a market forecast.

5. A proportional diffusion has an exact positive solution 01:01

An additive price model can become negative. To model proportional price changes, suppose instead that a non-dividend-paying stock satisfies

$$dS_t=\mu S_tdt+\sigma S_tdW_t,\qquad S_0>0,$$

with constant drift $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma>0$. This is geometric Brownian motion, or GBM. The drift is the instantaneous expected simple-return rate in this model; it is not the expected logarithmic-return rate. Both parameters are assumptions about the scenario distribution at this stage, not quantities inferred from the no-arbitrage principle.

Apply Itô's formula to $f(s)=\log s$. Its first two derivatives are $1/s$ and $-1/s^2$, so

$$d\log S_t=\left(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\right)dt+\sigma dW_t.$$

Integrating constant coefficients gives the explicit candidate

$$S_t=S_0\exp\left[\left(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\right)t+\sigma W_t\right].$$

The candidate is positive at every finite time and substitution through Itô's formula verifies the original SDE. This verification also avoids assuming the logarithm exists for an arbitrary candidate solution without justification. Standard SDE uniqueness conditions hold for these globally Lipschitz linear coefficients, so the verified solution is the model's unique strong solution.

There are not two competing exact models here. The SDE and its exponential solution describe the same process. A single Euler approximation, $S_{t+h}\approx S_t(1+\mu h+\sigma\sqrt h Z)$, is a numerical approximation that freezes the coefficients over the interval. It can produce a negative value. For $\mu=0$, $\sigma=1$, and $h=1$, its negative-price probability is $\Pr(Z<-1)\approx0.15865525$, while the exact GBM solution stays positive. Shrinking the step helps an approximation under its assumptions; it does not turn a coarse Gaussian price step into the exact transition law.

The ordinary return-compounding identity illustrates another distinction. If a price goes from 100 to 110 and then back to 100, the two simple returns are 0.10 and $-1/11$, not 0.10 and $-0.10$. Multiplying their gross returns gives $1.1(10/11)=1$, the correct zero total return. Their arithmetic average is positive because it averages changes relative to different bases. The log returns sum exactly to $\log(1.1)+\log(10/11)=0$. The Itô correction accounts for the accumulated second-order contribution when transforming stochastic prices; a coarse arithmetic average cannot replace that derivation.

6. Lognormal density, moments, and return conventions 16:20

At a horizon $T>0$, let $m=\log S_0+(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T$ and $v=\sigma^2T$. Then $\log S_T$ is normal with mean $m$ and variance $v$. The price is lognormal, meaning its logarithm is normal. For $s>0$, a change of variables gives its density

$$p(s,T)=\frac{1}{s\sqrt{2\pi v}} \exp\left[-\frac{(\log s-m)^2}{2v}\right].$$

The factor $1/s$ is the Jacobian $d(\log s)/ds$. Omitting it does not preserve total probability. Integrating the density after substituting $y=\log s$ reduces its normalization to the normal integral. A point threshold such as $s=100$ still has probability zero; a loss probability refers to an interval of prices.

For a normal $Z$, completing the square gives $\mathbb E[e^{uZ}]=e^{u^2/2}$. Substituting the lognormal representation yields $\mathbb E[S_T^k]=\exp(km+k^2v/2)$ for any real $k$ for which we use the formula here. In particular,

$$\mathbb E[S_T]=S_0e^{\mu T},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_T)=S_0^2e^{2\mu T}(e^{\sigma^2T}-1).$$

The moment calculation's decisive exponent identity is

$$ky-\frac{(y-m)^2}{2v}=-\frac{(y-m-kv)^2}{2v}+km+\frac{k^2v}{2}.$$

After taking the constant factor outside the integral, the remaining normal density has shifted mean $m+kv$ and unchanged variance $v$. It integrates to one. For $k=2$, the resulting second moment is $e^{2m+2v}$. Subtracting $e^{2m+v}$, the square of the first moment, produces the displayed variance. This calculation distinguishes the variance of log price $v$ from the variance of price itself.

The median is $e^m=S_0e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}$, below the mean when $\sigma>0$. For the synthetic parameters $S_0=100$, $\mu=0.08$, $\sigma=0.2$, and $T=1$, the mean is 108.32870677, median 106.18365465, and variance 478.91887168. The probability of a price loss is

$$\Pr(S_T<S_0)=\Phi\left(\frac{-(\mu-\sigma^2/2)\sqrt T}{\sigma}\right) \approx0.38208858,$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. An eight-percent drift does not imply that prices rise on every path, nor that the median return is eight percent.

The simple return is $R_T=S_T/S_0-1$. The log return is $\ell_T=\log(S_T/S_0)$. Log returns add over adjacent intervals, while simple returns compound multiplicatively: $1+R_{0,T}=(1+R_{0,u})(1+R_{u,T})$. Under GBM, $\ell_T$ has mean $(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T$ and variance $\sigma^2T$; the annualized log return $\ell_T/T$ has variance $\sigma^2/T$. The stock price's variance and the log return's variance are different quantities with different units.

7. The forward density equation provides an independent description 17:11

For a smooth compactly supported test function $g$, Itô's formula and a zero-mean stochastic-integral term give

$$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[g(X_t)] =\mathbb E\left[a(t,X_t)g'(X_t)+\tfrac12b(t,X_t)^2g''(X_t)\right].$$

Suppose $X_t$ has a sufficiently regular density $p(x,t)$ and differentiation under the integral is permitted. Replace expectations by integrals and transfer derivatives from $g$ to the coefficient-density products by integration by parts. Compact support eliminates the boundary terms for this derivation. Since the identity holds for every such test function, the density satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation in the weak sense,

$$\partial_t p=-\partial_x(ap)+\tfrac12\partial_{xx}(b^2p).$$

When all terms are smooth, the equation holds pointwise as well. For GBM it becomes $\partial_tp=-\partial_s(\mu sp)+\tfrac12\partial_{ss}(\sigma^2s^2p)$ on $s>0$. Transforming to log price turns it into the constant-drift diffusion equation already solved. Transforming the density back introduces the same $1/s$ Jacobian. Thus the SDE solution and the forward equation agree for different reasons.

Changing from left-point to another stochastic-integral convention without changing the drift generally changes the model. For multiplicative noise, an Itô equation and a Stratonovich equation need a compensating drift conversion to describe the same process. We use the Itô convention throughout; no reinterpretation of the unchanged symbols is implied.

8. A terminal cash test is not a survival guarantee 32:12

Let a firm's cash balance be $C_t=c+\mu_Ct+\sigma_CW_t$, where $c>0$ is initial cash, $\mu_C$ is cash drift per year, and $\sigma_C$ is cash volatility per square-root year. The terminal insolvency event is $\{C_T\le0\}$. The event of exhausting cash at any time is $\{\min_{0\le t\le T}C_t\le0\}$. The former event is contained in the latter, but the reverse need not hold: a path can cross zero and then recover.

For a transparent zero-drift example, take $\mu_C=0$, $\sigma_C=2$ million currency units per square-root year, and $T=1$. The terminal-negative probability is $\Phi[-c/(\sigma_C\sqrt T)]$. Setting it to five percent requires $c=3.28970725$ million.

The Brownian reflection principle gives the crossing probability as twice the terminal tail, hence ten percent here. The principle reflects the segment after the first boundary crossing: paths that cross and return above the boundary correspond to equally likely reflected paths ending below it, by symmetry and the strong Markov property at the first-passage time. The strong Markov theorem restarts Brownian motion at a stopping time; deterministic-time independence alone would not justify this step. This standard theorem is invoked here; see Lalley, Brownian-motion notes, section 2.3, Propositions 3–4. The terminal-negative paths supply the other half of the crossing event. This is a Brownian-model identity with a continuous boundary and zero drift, not a formula to transfer unchanged to arbitrary cash processes.

An endpoint insolvency constraint permits temporary shortfalls followed by recovery. Monitoring dates, credit lines, early liquidation, and payment jumps can all alter a real survival question. Specifying the event is as important as calculating its probability.

9. Executable checks and limits

The chapter laboratory checks the normal second and fourth moments by Gaussian quadrature, the finite square-expansion identity on a seeded Gaussian path, and lognormal mean/variance by independent integration over the normal driver. It also tests the forward density PDE through finite differences at three price levels and computes the endpoint and crossing probabilities in the cash example.

On its stated seeded path the finite quadratic sum is 1.02575775 and the left-point sum is −0.06727407; the endpoint is 0.94403898. Their exact finite identity holds to floating-point tolerance. The quadratic sum is not forced to equal one at finite resolution. Its theoretical variance is 0.125 for 16 intervals and 0.00048828 for 4096 intervals, illustrating which uncertainty shrinks in the limit.

These checks support the synthetic examples and implementations. They do not establish that market prices follow GBM, that estimated volatility remains constant, or that normal tails describe a liquidity crisis. The probability calculations in this chapter use the physical scenario drift. Pricing under a different probability measure is introduced separately, after replication supplies the reason for it.

Exercises

  1. Solve $\partial_t\rho=\tfrac12\partial_{xx}\rho$ from a unit point mass by Fourier transformation. Identify the conditions used to discard boundary terms.
  2. Derive $\mathbb E[Q_n]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Q_n)$ for equal Brownian time steps. Explain why a single squared increment does not become equal to its mean in relative terms.
  3. Derive $\int_0^TW_t\,dW_t$ from a finite square expansion. Compute its expectation, variance, and a lower bound on its support.
  4. Apply Itô's formula to $\log S_t$ for GBM and derive the price mean, price median, and annualized log-return variance. Explain why the three are not interchangeable summaries.
  5. Derive the lognormal density's Jacobian factor and obtain the GBM Fokker–Planck equation from a test-function identity.
  6. A zero-drift cash model has initial cash $c$, volatility $\sigma_C$, and horizon $T$. Derive the buffer giving a five-percent terminal-negative probability, then compute its continuous-time zero-crossing probability. Explain which event a treasury policy must specify.
Fully worked solutions

  1. Fourier transformation changes $\partial_{xx}\rho$ to $-k^2\widehat\rho$ under sufficient decay and integrability. Thus $\partial_t\widehat\rho=-k^2\widehat\rho/2$. The point-mass initial condition gives $\widehat\rho(k,0)=1$, so $\widehat\rho=e^{-k^2t/2}$. Fourier inversion gives the density $e^{-x^2/(2t)}/\sqrt{2\pi t}$. Spatial boundary terms vanish because the Gaussian and its derivatives decay at both infinities for $t>0$. The initial condition is weak convergence to a point mass, not an ordinary density value at $t=0$.

  2. Each $\Delta W_j$ is $\sqrt h Z_j$ with $h=T/n$. Its square has mean $h$ and variance $h^2(3-1)=2h^2$. Independence permits summing these variances, giving $\mathbb E Q_n=T$ and $\operatorname{Var}Q_n=2T^2/n$. For one square, the ratio of standard deviation to mean is $\sqrt{2h^2}/h=\sqrt2$ regardless of $h$. For the full sum it is $\sqrt{2/n}$, which tends to zero. The aggregation step is essential.

  3. Summing $W_{t_{j+1}}^2-W_{t_j}^2=2W_{t_j}\Delta W_j+(\Delta W_j)^2$ gives the exact finite identity. The left sum converges to the Itô integral and the square sum to $T$, so $I=(W_T^2-T)/2$. Since $W_T$ is normal with variance $T$, its square has mean $T$ and variance $2T^2$. Therefore $\mathbb EI=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}I=T^2/2$. Since $W_T^2\ge0$, one has $I\ge-T/2$. A nondegenerate normal variable has no such lower support bound, so $I$ is not normal.

  4. The derivatives of $\log s$ are $1/s$ and $-1/s^2$, producing drift $\mu-\sigma^2/2$ and diffusion coefficient $\sigma$. Integration gives $\log S_T=\log S_0+(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T+\sigma W_T$. The exponential normal moment gives $\mathbb E S_T=S_0e^{\mu T}$, while symmetry of the normal log price makes the median $S_0e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}$. Dividing a log return by $T$ divides its variance by $T^2$, giving $\sigma^2/T$. The mean price, median price, and variance of an annualized log return describe different random quantities.

  5. If $Y=\log S$ has density $f_Y$, probability preservation gives $f_S(s)ds=f_Y(\log s)d\log s=f_Y(\log s)ds/s$ for $s>0$. This yields the $1/s$ factor. For a smooth compact-support $g$, Itô's formula gives $d\mathbb Eg(S_t)/dt=\int[\mu s g'(s)+\sigma^2s^2g''(s)/2]p(s,t)ds$. Integration by parts once in the first term and twice in the second yields $\int g[-\partial_s(\mu sp)+\partial_{ss}(\sigma^2s^2p)/2]ds$, establishing the weak forward equation. Boundary/integrability conditions are needed to promote this to a pointwise statement.

  6. The standardized terminal balance has threshold $-c/(\sigma_C\sqrt T)$. If $z_{0.05}=\Phi^{-1}(0.05)$, the required buffer is $c=-z_{0.05}\sigma_C\sqrt T$. With $\sigma_C=2$ and $T=1$, this is 3.28970725 million. Reflection gives a crossing probability $2\Phi[-c/(\sigma_C\sqrt T)]=0.10$. A policy concerning inability to pay before the horizon needs a crossing or dated-cash-flow event; an endpoint solvency constraint alone permits negative balances followed by recovery. Nonzero drift or jumps requires the corresponding different hitting analysis.

"""Quadratic-variation identities and independently integrated GBM moments."""
from math import exp, isclose, log, pi, sqrt

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import ndtr


def lognormal_parameters(spot, drift, volatility, horizon):
    values = [spot, drift, volatility, horizon]
    if not all(np.isfinite(x) for x in values):
        raise ValueError("parameters must be finite")
    if spot <= 0 or volatility <= 0 or horizon <= 0:
        raise ValueError("spot, volatility, and horizon must be positive")
    return log(spot) + (drift - volatility ** 2 / 2) * horizon, \
        volatility * sqrt(horizon)


def gbm_density(price, spot, drift, volatility, horizon):
    if price <= 0:
        return 0.0
    location, scale = lognormal_parameters(spot, drift, volatility, horizon)
    z = (log(price) - location) / scale
    return exp(-z * z / 2) / (price * scale * sqrt(2 * pi))


def main():
    # Gaussian quadrature checks normal fourth moments independently.
    nodes, weights = np.polynomial.hermite.hermgauss(40)
    z, weights = sqrt(2) * nodes, weights / sqrt(pi)
    assert isclose(float(weights @ (z ** 2)), 1, abs_tol=1e-13)
    assert isclose(float(weights @ (z ** 4)), 3, abs_tol=1e-12)
    assert isclose(2 / (4096 * 0.1 ** 2), 0.048828125, abs_tol=1e-15)
    normalized_exponential = np.exp(0.3 * z - 0.3 ** 2 / 2)
    assert isclose(float(weights @ normalized_exponential), 1,
                   abs_tol=1e-13)
    assert isclose(1.1 * (10 / 11), 1, abs_tol=1e-15)
    # A genuinely random adapted step integrand: two independent increments.
    dw1, dw2 = np.meshgrid(z * sqrt(.5), z * sqrt(.5), indexing='ij')
    joint_weights = weights[:, None] * weights[None, :]
    integral = dw1 + (1 + dw1) * dw2
    variance_by_isometry = .5 + .5 * float(weights @ (1 + z * sqrt(.5))**2)
    assert isclose(float(np.sum(joint_weights * integral)), 0,
                   abs_tol=1e-13)
    assert isclose(float(np.sum(joint_weights * integral**2)), 1.25,
                   abs_tol=1e-12)
    assert isclose(variance_by_isometry, 1.25, abs_tol=1e-13)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(1701)
    steps, horizon = 4096, 1.0
    increments = rng.normal(0, sqrt(horizon / steps), size=steps)
    path = np.r_[0.0, np.cumsum(increments)]
    left_integral = float(path[:-1] @ increments)
    quadratic_sum = float(increments @ increments)
    assert isclose(path[-1] ** 2, 2 * left_integral + quadratic_sum,
                   abs_tol=2e-13)
    print(f"sample quadratic sum={quadratic_sum:.8f}; "
          f"left sum={left_integral:.8f}; endpoint={path[-1]:.8f}")
    for count in [16, 256, 4096]:
        expectation = horizon
        variance = 2 * horizon ** 2 / count
        print(f"n={count}: E[QV]={expectation:.6f}, "
              f"Var[QV]={variance:.8f}")
    spot, drift, volatility, horizon = 100, 0.08, 0.2, 1.0
    location, scale = lognormal_parameters(spot, drift, volatility, horizon)
    prices = np.exp(location + scale * z)
    mean_numeric = float(weights @ prices)
    second_numeric = float(weights @ (prices ** 2))
    mean = spot * exp(drift * horizon)
    variance = spot ** 2 * exp(2 * drift * horizon) * (
        exp(volatility ** 2 * horizon) - 1)
    assert isclose(mean_numeric, mean, rel_tol=1e-12)
    assert isclose(second_numeric - mean_numeric ** 2, variance,
                   rel_tol=1e-11)
    median = exp(location)
    loss_probability = float(ndtr((log(spot) - location) / scale))
    print(f"GBM mean={mean:.8f}, median={median:.8f}, "
          f"variance={variance:.8f}, P[loss]={loss_probability:.8f}")
    # Independent finite-difference test of the forward density PDE.
    for price in [80, 100, 130]:
        dx, dt = price * 1e-4, 1e-4

        def density(s, t):
            return gbm_density(
                s, spot, drift, volatility, t)
        time_derivative = (density(price, 1 + dt)
                           - density(price, 1 - dt)) / (2 * dt)
        flux_plus = drift * (price + dx) * density(price + dx, 1)
        flux_minus = drift * (price - dx) * density(price - dx, 1)

        def diffusion(s):
            return volatility ** 2 * s ** 2 * density(s, 1)
        rhs = -(flux_plus - flux_minus) / (2 * dx) + (
            diffusion(price + dx) - 2 * diffusion(price)
            + diffusion(price - dx)) / (2 * dx ** 2)
        assert isclose(time_derivative, rhs, rel_tol=2e-5, abs_tol=1e-8)
    negative_euler = float(ndtr(-1.0))
    assert isclose(negative_euler, 0.15865525393145707, abs_tol=1e-14)
    from scipy.special import ndtri
    cash_buffer = -2 * float(ndtri(0.05))
    endpoint_risk = float(ndtr(-cash_buffer / 2))
    hitting_risk = 2 * endpoint_risk
    assert isclose(endpoint_risk, 0.05, abs_tol=1e-13)
    assert isclose(hitting_risk, 0.10, abs_tol=1e-13)
    print(f"zero-drift cash buffer={cash_buffer:.8f}: "
          f"endpoint risk={endpoint_risk:.4f}, "
          f"hitting risk={hitting_risk:.4f}")
    print("Ito identity, Gaussian moments, GBM and tail checks passed")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
