13. Option cash flows and put–call parity

A risk manager is offered a call and a put on the same non-dividend-paying share, both expiring on the same date with strike 100. The call costs eight and the put costs three. Without choosing a volatility model, can the manager determine whether those premiums are consistent with the share and funding prices? Yes: their difference replicates a linear obligation, even though each option separately has a nonlinear payoff.

We use one share as the unit of account and keep all premiums and payoffs in the same currency. The numerical case has spot price 100 and a discount factor of 0.95 to expiry. Those are synthetic inputs chosen to make the replication transparent. The chapter first establishes the payment rules, then identifies the precise relationship that connects the option premiums.

1. Rights, obligations, exercise dates, and moneyness 00:24

Let $S_t$ be the share price at time $t$, $K\ge0$ the contractual strike price, and $T$ the expiry date. We assume a nonnegative stock price, $S_t\ge0$. This stock-domain assumption supplies some later loss bounds; it is not a theorem that every conceivable commodity or derivative quotation must be nonnegative.

A call gives its holder the right to buy the specified share at $K$ under its exercise terms. A put gives the right to sell it at $K$. A European option can be exercised at its specified expiry, whereas an American option permits exercise during its contractual exercise window. The writer is the counterparty responsible for performing the corresponding obligation upon valid exercise. The premium is the price paid to acquire the right.

We write $c_0$ and $p_0$ for European call and put premiums today. “Long” means owning the option; “short” means having written it. A short call is not a long put: one is an obligation to sell when exercised, while the other is a right to sell. Their payoff slopes, premium cash flows, and loss limits differ.

A call is in the money when $S_t>K$, out of the money when $S_t<K$, and at the money when they are equal, with market usage sometimes applying the last phrase to a nearby strike. For a put the inequalities reverse. Moneyness compares price with strike. It does not incorporate the premium, financing, or whether exercise is permitted today.

The immediate-exercise amount is $(S_t-K)^+$ for a call and $(K-S_t)^+$ for a put, where $x^+=\max(x,0)$. These amounts are often called intrinsic values. For a European contract before expiry, they describe a hypothetical exercise amount, not an available withdrawal from the option. That difference matters when comparing current premium with intrinsic value.

The Options Industry Council's basic definitions provide an institutional reference for holder rights, writer obligations, and premium. Product-specific exercise, settlement, and adjustment rules still need their actual contracts. The abstract payoff formulas below assume a matched underlying unit and cash-equivalent valuation of the exercise exchange.

2. Four elementary payoffs and their financed profits 09:09

At expiry, exercising a call when $S_T>K$ buys a share for less than its market value. If $S_T\le K$, allowing the right to lapse is at least as good. Consequently its terminal payoff is $(S_T-K)^+$. The put payoff follows by reversing purchase and sale: $(K-S_T)^+$. The short sides owe the negatives of those payoffs.

Position Initial premium cash flow Expiry payoff
Long call $-c_0$ $(S_T-K)^+$
Short call $+c_0$ $-(S_T-K)^+$
Long put $-p_0$ $(K-S_T)^+$
Short put $+p_0$ $-(K-S_T)^+$

The table separates dates. Let $P(0,T)>0$ denote the current price of a default-free claim paying one currency unit at expiry, and assume the same factor can be used for the stated premium financing comparison. A premium paid today has terminal equivalent $c_0/P(0,T)$ or $p_0/P(0,T)$. Thus the long-call terminal profit is

$$\Pi_C=(S_T-K)^+-\frac{c_0}{P(0,T)},$$

and the long-put terminal profit is $\Pi_P=(K-S_T)^+-p_0/P(0,T)$. Profit here refers to the standalone position under the financing assumption; business income from another exposure is not silently included. With borrowing/lending spreads, substitute the actual financing arrangement rather than a common idealized factor.

In the running case, $K=100$, $c_0=8$, $p_0=3$, and $P(0,T)=0.95$. The call's financed premium is 8.421052632. Its profit is negative at $S_T=105$ despite a positive payoff of five. Break-even occurs at $S_T=108.421052632$. The put's financed premium is 3.157894737, so its break-even is 96.842105263. Neither threshold is the strike itself.

For a positive premium, a long call can lose its financed premium and can gain without bound if the model permits unbounded stock prices. Its writer has the opposite profile. The long put's payoff cannot exceed $K$ when $S_T\ge0$, so its maximum standalone terminal profit is $K-p_0/P(0,T)$; its writer's worst terminal loss has that corresponding finite bound. Stating that every option writer has unlimited loss would therefore be false in this stock model.

Long and short contractual payoffs sum to zero state by state. That statement does not imply that hedging provides no economic benefit: the two parties can begin with different business exposures and preferences. Nor does it imply zero combined social cost after transaction fees, funding spreads, credit losses, or market impact are introduced.

3. Payoff ordering gives weak strike monotonicity 21:27

Take two otherwise identical European calls with strikes $K_1<K_2$. For every terminal stock price,

$$0\le(S_T-K_1)^+-(S_T-K_2)^+\le K_2-K_1.$$

Below $K_1$ both terms vanish. Between the strikes, the difference is $S_T-K_1$, ranging from zero to the strike gap. Above $K_2$, the difference is exactly $K_2-K_1$. In a frictionless market with feasible offsetting positions, statewise payoff dominance implies that the lower-strike call cannot cost less. Therefore $c(K_1)\ge c(K_2)$.

The inequality is weak, not automatically strict. If neither option can ever pay in the permitted state model, both can have zero value. Similarly, $p(K_2)\ge p(K_1)$ for otherwise matched puts because the higher strike never reduces a put's terminal payment. The price difference for these European vertical spreads is also bounded above by $(K_2-K_1)P(0,T)$, the cost of a sure expiry payment equal to the strike gap.

This ordering is a no-arbitrage comparison rather than a prediction about the future share price. It does not require specifying how likely each possible terminal price is. In contrast, deciding whether a call is attractive for a particular investor requires its premium, the investor's exposure, and the distribution or objective used in that decision. A payoff that rises with stock price does not prove that every bullish investor should choose that contract rather than stock, a spread, or another position.

4. The nonlinear pieces combine into a linear identity 04:47

For the same strike and expiry, subtracting the put payoff from the call payoff gives

$$ (S_T-K)^+-(K-S_T)^+=S_T-K.$$

When $S_T\ge K$, the put term is zero and the call supplies the right-hand side. When $S_T<K$, the call is zero and subtracting the put gives $-(K-S_T)=S_T-K$. The equality also holds at $S_T=K$. This is a pathwise algebraic identity; it does not depend on normal returns, constant volatility, or risk-neutral probabilities.

One long call and one short put therefore replicate a long-share position financed by an obligation to pay $K$ at $T$, provided the share has no intervening income or costs in this model. The cost today of the share-and-borrowing portfolio is $S_0-KP(0,T)$. Applying the law of one price gives European put–call parity:

$$c_0-p_0=S_0-KP(0,T),\qquad c_0+KP(0,T)=p_0+S_0.$$

For the alternative positive-payoff proof, a call plus an expiry cash payment $K$ yields $\max(S_T,K)$, while a put plus one share yields the same amount. Equality of their current costs gives the second form directly. The proof assumes matched underlying, strike, expiry, settlement units, European exercise, default performance, and feasible frictionless trading/financing. A mismatch in one attribute is a different portfolio comparison.

The running premiums obey the relation because $8-3=100-100(0.95)=5$. It follows that once the call, stock, strike, and discount factor are given, a matching European put must cost three under this model. Parity does not determine the call and put separately: many pairs with the same difference can satisfy it while encoding different uncertainty. A full option-pricing model supplies additional information, developed later through binomial replication and continuous-time dynamics.

Campbell Harvey's Duke option-valuation notes, in the put–call parity discussion, develop the same portfolio-comparison method. The dated notes are used for the mathematical argument, not their historical exchange listings or product examples. Our proof supplies the financing and contract-match assumptions locally.

5. A parity discrepancy needs an executable cash-flow ledger 16:52

Suppose the call premium becomes nine while the put remains three, the stock 100, and the strike cash claim 95. The call-plus-bond side costs $9+95=104$, while put-plus-stock costs $3+100=103$. Sell the expensive payoff package and buy the cheaper one. Explicitly, write the call, borrow 95 with repayment 100 at $T$, buy the put, and buy one share.

The initial signed cash flow is $+9+95-3-100=+1$. Its terminal cash flow is

$$S_T+(K-S_T)^+-(S_T-K)^+-K=0.$$

Below the strike the put converts the share into $K$ and the call expires. Above the strike the call obligation absorbs $S_T-K$, leaving $K$ from share value; that pays the borrowing. Thus the strategy retains a positive initial receipt without a terminal liability. The reverse discrepancy requires the reverse positions, including a feasible short sale of stock with usable proceeds.

Every leg has to be executable at the price actually used. If the displayed call midprice is nine but its executable bid is eight, the put ask is four, and the stock ask is 100.50, the same proposed ledger yields $8+95-4-100.50=-1.50$ before any additional costs. A positive discrepancy between midquotes has not established an arbitrage. Bid–ask spreads, borrowing fees, settlement constraints, dividends owed on short stock, and margin liquidity can change the opportunity set.

The OIC parity discussion explicitly distinguishes its frictionless borrowing and transaction assumptions from real trading restrictions. For rigorous pricing work, retain the statewise ledger rather than replacing it with a statement that market pressure will eventually correct an apparent gap. The ledger identifies exactly which fees or constraints can invalidate the proposed trade.

6. Dividends must be matched at their actual dates 23:48

Suppose the share pays known cash dividends $D_j$ at dates $t_j<T$, and write $I_0=\sum_jD_jP(0,t_j)$ for their present value under the same frictionless funding framework. One way to isolate the terminal share is to buy it and borrow each dividend's present value. At its payment date, use the dividend to repay that matching obligation. The net initial cost of this prepaid terminal-share exposure is $S_0-I_0$.

Replacing the no-income share position in the parity proof gives

$$c_0-p_0=S_0-I_0-KP(0,T).$$

Equivalently, call plus strike cash claim plus dividend-date cash claims matches put plus the share. At each dividend date the cash claim on the first side pays exactly what the share on the second side pays. At expiry both sides pay $\max(S_T,K)$. This proof matches the intermediate dates directly; it does not hide dividends inside an undated terminal adjustment.

For $S_0=100$, $I_0=2$, and $KP(0,T)=95$, the difference is three. Synthetic premiums $c_0=7$ and $p_0=4$ obey the dividend-adjusted identity. Applying the no-dividend difference of five would report a false discrepancy because the stock leg contains an income stream the option-only leg lacks.

The dividend must be known for this exact cash-claim replication. Replacing it by an uncertain forecast dividend without modeling its risk changes the argument. A proportional dividend-yield model also uses a different replicating construction, not an instruction to subtract any guessed annual percentage times spot. The contract must specify which stock and dividend entitlements the options reference.

7. Time value is not always a nonnegative residual for a European option

The commonly reported time value is premium minus current intrinsic amount. For an American option, immediate exercise is available, so an option worth less than that exercise amount would violate its exercise right under frictionless conditions. A European option lacks that right before expiry. Its premium can therefore be less than a hypothetical immediate-exercise amount without creating an arbitrage.

A deterministic counterexample makes the mechanism visible. Let a non-dividend share cost 50, with deterministic evolution $S_T=50e^{0.1}$ over one year and a matching continuously compounded funding rate of ten percent. Let a European put have strike 100. Its payoff is certainly $100-50e^{0.1}$, so its present value is $100e^{-0.1}-50=40.483741804$. Current intrinsic amount is 50. The difference is negative because the European put pays the strike only later.

This construction satisfies put–call parity: the call cannot pay on the deterministic path, so $c_0=0$, and $-p_0=S_0-Ke^{-0.1}$. There is no free opportunity to collect 50 today from the European put. The example does not say all puts have negative time value; it disproves a universal nonnegative-residual claim for European contracts.

Nor is option value merely the physical probability of finishing in the money. Payoff magnitude, payment time, and the pricing of states matter. An event with high probability but tiny payment can be less valuable than a rarer large payment. Later pricing chapters distinguish the physical distribution used for forecasts from the state-price or risk-neutral construction implied by replication.

Exercises

  1. Derive the long-call, short-call, long-put, and short-put expiry payoffs. Under $S_T\ge0$, identify which losses can be unbounded and which are bounded by strike or premium under the stated funding model.
  2. With strike 100, call premium eight, put premium three, and discount factor 0.95, derive both standalone terminal-profit break-evens. Give an in-the-money outcome for the call that still produces a loss.
  3. Prove $(S_T-K)^+-(K-S_T)^+=S_T-K$ by considering both strike regions. Use it to derive European put–call parity and list the contract attributes that must match.
  4. Change the call premium to nine while keeping the running put, stock, and funding prices. Give the complete initial and terminal arbitrage ledger. Recalculate its initial receipt using call bid eight, put ask four, and stock ask 100.50.
  5. A share pays known dividends with present value two. Its price is 100 and the strike cash claim costs 95. Derive the required call-minus-put premium difference. Show how the dividend is matched at its payment date.
  6. In the deterministic stock example with spot 50, strike 100, and a one-year ten-percent continuously compounded rate, compute the European put price and current intrinsic amount. Explain why their ordering does not imply an arbitrage.
Fully worked solutions

  1. Exercising a call is beneficial at expiry only when $S_T>K$, giving $(S_T-K)^+$. The put gives $(K-S_T)^+$; each writer owes its negative. The standalone long option loses at most its financed premium. A naked short call has unbounded potential loss when the model permits arbitrarily large stock prices. A short put's maximum payoff obligation is $K$ because stock cannot fall below zero in this model; subtracting financed premium receipts gives its bounded terminal-profit loss. These limits require the stated stock domain and do not describe all commodity-price models.

  2. The call profit is $(S_T-100)^+-8/0.95$, so break-even is 108.421052632. The put profit is $(100-S_T)^+-3/0.95$, so break-even is 96.842105263. At $S_T=105$, the call is in the money and pays five, but its financed cost is about 8.421053; standalone profit is about -3.421053. Moneyness and financed profitability answer different questions.

  3. Above the strike, call minus put is $S_T-K-0$; below it the difference is $0-(K-S_T)=S_T-K$. At equality all terms vanish. One long share plus borrowing with repayment $K$ has this same payoff, so its initial cost $S_0-KP(0,T)$ equals $c_0-p_0$. The options must match underlying quantity/unit, strike, expiry, European exercise, and settlement terms. The supporting trading, funding, and default assumptions must also apply to both portfolios.

  4. Buy stock and put, write the call, and borrow 95 repaying 100. Today the receipt is $9+95-3-100=1$. At expiry, stock plus put minus call minus borrowing gives $S_T+(100-S_T)^+-(S_T-100)^+-100=0$. With executable prices, the initial amount is $8+95-4-100.50=-1.50$, so that proposed trade no longer has a positive initial surplus. Funding fees and other frictions would need separate inclusion.

  5. Finance each known dividend by borrowing its present value and repay the loan from the dividend received. The remaining prepaid cost of the terminal share is $100-2=98$. The parity difference is therefore $98-95=3$. Equivalently, add dividend-date cash claims to the call-plus-strike package so its intermediate payments equal the dividends from put-plus-stock. Exact dated matching, rather than a general verbal dividend adjustment, supplies the result.

  6. The deterministic share at expiry is $50e^{0.1}<100$. Discounting the put's known payoff gives $e^{-0.1}(100-50e^{0.1})=40.483741804$. Current intrinsic amount is 50, but immediate exercise is not allowed for this European contract. The gap compensates for receiving the strike at the later date. The call has value zero and parity holds, so the example is consistent with the stated no-arbitrage market.

"""European payoff, financed-profit and parity cash-flow certificates."""
from math import exp, isclose, isfinite


def payoffs(spot, strike):
    if not all(isfinite(x) and x >= 0 for x in [spot, strike]):
        raise ValueError("Nonnegative finite stock and strike required")
    return max(spot - strike, 0), max(strike - spot, 0)


def main():
    strike, discount, call_price, put_price, initial = 100, 0.95, 8, 3, 100
    assert isclose(call_price - put_price, initial - strike * discount)
    for spot in [0, 30, 80, 99.999, 100, 100.001, 110, 140, 1000]:
        call, put = payoffs(spot, strike)
        assert isclose(call - put, spot - strike, abs_tol=1e-12)
        assert isclose(call + strike, spot + put)
        call_profit, put_profit = call - call_price / \
            discount, put - put_price / discount
        assert isclose(call_profit + (-call + call_price / discount), 0)
        assert isclose(put_profit + (-put + put_price / discount), 0)
        # Expensive call-and-bond side: buy stock+put, short call+bond.
        assert isclose(spot + put - call - strike, 0)
    call_break_even = strike + call_price / discount
    put_break_even = strike - put_price / discount
    assert isclose(payoffs(call_break_even, strike)[0], call_price / discount)
    assert isclose(payoffs(put_break_even, strike)[1], put_price / discount)
    rich_call = 9
    receipt = rich_call + strike * discount - put_price - initial
    assert receipt == 1
    executable_receipt = 8 + 95 - 4 - 100.5
    assert executable_receipt < 0
    dividend_pv, call_dividend, put_dividend = 2, 7, 4
    assert (call_dividend - put_dividend
            == initial - dividend_pv - strike * discount)
    for spot in range(0, 201):
        low_call = payoffs(spot, 90)[0]
        high_call = payoffs(spot, 110)[0]
        assert 0 <= low_call - high_call <= 20
        low_put = payoffs(spot, 90)[1]
        high_put = payoffs(spot, 110)[1]
        assert 0 <= high_put - low_put <= 20
    s0, k, rate, maturity = 50, 100, 0.1, 1
    terminal = s0 * exp(rate * maturity)
    deterministic_put = exp(-rate * maturity) * max(k - terminal, 0)
    assert isclose(deterministic_put, k * exp(-rate * maturity) - s0)
    assert deterministic_put < k - s0
    print(
        f"call break-even={call_break_even:.9f}; "
        f"put break-even={put_break_even:.9f}")
    print(f"parity arbitrage initial receipt={receipt:.6f}; final payoff=0")
    print(f"bid-ask adjusted receipt={executable_receipt:.6f}")
    print(f"deterministic European put={deterministic_put:.9f}; intrinsic=50")
    print("payoff, financed profit, strike order and parity checks passed")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
