18. Binomial replication and risk-neutral valuation

An uncertain cash flow does not acquire a unique price merely because its mean can be calculated. A mean requires a probability law; a discount rate requires an economic justification. The binomial market supplies a stronger argument. Two traded assets span the two possible future states, so every terminal cash flow has an exactly matching traded portfolio. Its cost fixes the derivative price by the law of one price.

This chapter develops that argument before introducing a pricing probability. The distinction matters: a risk-neutral probability is a representation of traded prices, and does not assert that investors disregard risk. Throughout, prices and cash flows are in one currency, stock dividends are absent, borrowing and lending use the same deterministic rate, trading is frictionless, short sales are allowed with usable proceeds, and contracts are fulfilled. These are model assumptions rather than claims about actual market access.

1. Two states and two traded assets 06:34

Let the current stock price be \(S_0>0\). After one interval of length \(h\) years, it is either \(S_0u\) or \(S_0d\), where \(u>d>0\). The quantities \(u,d\) are gross price factors: \(u=1.2\) means a 20% increase; \(d=0.8\) means a 20% decrease. A cash investment of one currency unit becomes \(R=e^{rh}\), where \(r\) is the continuously compounded annual rate. We sometimes specify \(R\) directly, which avoids confusing an effective interval return with an annual continuous rate.

Assume both states have positive physical probability. Absence of arbitrage requires

\[ d<R<u. \]

For example, if \(R\ge u\), short one share and invest the proceeds \(S_0\). Initial net cost is zero; terminal wealth is \(S_0(R-u)\) in the up state and \(S_0(R-d)\) in the down state. Both are nonnegative and the second is positive. The equality \(R=u\) still permits arbitrage because the down state is possible. If \(R\le d\), borrowing \(S_0\) to buy a share gives the reverse construction, with a strictly positive up-state gain. Strict inequalities encode the possibility of both states, not a numerical convenience.

Consider a derivative with terminal payments \(H_u,H_d\). Hold \(\Delta\) shares and \(b\) currency units in cash initially. Negative \(b\) means borrowing. Matching each state gives two linear equations:

\[ \Delta S_0u+bR=H_u, \qquad \Delta S_0d+bR=H_d. \]

Subtracting and substituting yield

\[ \Delta=\frac{H_u-H_d}{S_0(u-d)}, \qquad b=\frac{uH_d-dH_u}{R(u-d)}. \]

The coefficient \(\Delta\) has units shares per derivative; \(b\) has units currency per derivative. Their initial cost is \(V_0=\Delta S_0+b\). If the quoted derivative price exceeds this cost, sell the derivative and buy the replicating portfolio. Their future obligations cancel in every state, leaving a positive receipt now. If the quote is lower, reverse both positions. Financing the initial receipt in cash makes this an arbitrage with zero initial net investment. Thus replication determines the price without an assumption about expected stock returns. The original Cox–Ross–Rubinstein paper, sections 2–3, manuscript pages 3–6 develops this same two-state economic argument.

The lecture sometimes forms a portfolio containing a long derivative plus a stock hedge. In that convention its stock holding is \(-\Delta\). There is no contradiction with a positive call replication delta: one portfolio reproduces the call, while the other removes the risk of an existing long call.

2. Pricing probabilities are implied weights 15:00

Rearrange the replication cost:

\[ V_0=\frac{qH_u+(1-q)H_d}{R}, \qquad q=\frac{R-d}{u-d}. \]

The no-arbitrage inequalities make \(0<q<1\). Therefore these algebraic weights can be treated as probabilities under a measure denoted \(\mathbb Q\). They price the stock correctly because

\[ qS_0u+(1-q)S_0d=S_0R. \]

Equivalently, the discounted stock is a martingale under \(\mathbb Q\): its conditional expected future discounted value equals its present discounted value. A martingale statement concerns a conditional mean, not the realized path. Under \(\mathbb Q\), the stock remains risky; both terminal values still occur with positive probability.

The state prices \(\pi_u=q/R\) and \(\pi_d=(1-q)/R\) give an alternative interpretation. A claim paying one currency unit only in the up state costs \(\pi_u\); the corresponding down-state claim costs \(\pi_d\). Adding the two claims gives one certain future currency unit, priced at \(1/R\). Dividing state prices by that discount factor normalizes them into probabilities. This identifies exactly what is constructed from market prices.

For a synthetic example, take \(S_0=100,u=1.2,d=0.8,R=1.05\), and a call with strike \(K=100\). Its payoffs are 20 and zero. Then

\[ q=0.625,\quad \Delta=0.5,\quad b=-38.09523810,\quad c_0=11.90476190. \]

The portfolio costs \(50-38.09523810\). At maturity its debt is 40; stock holdings are worth 60 or 40, leaving precisely 20 or zero. This calculation verifies the full statewise replication rather than only the expected payoff.

Suppose the physical up probability is instead \(p=0.8\). The physical expected terminal stock price is 112, whereas its pricing expectation is 105. Both statements can hold. Actual expected stock return is 12% over the interval; the bank return is 5%; no arbitrage does not force these expectations to agree. The physical expected call payoff is 16, whose discount at the bank factor would be \(16/1.05=15.23809524\). That number is not its replicating cost. Discounting an unhedged risky mean at the risk-free rate is the unjustified step.

The economic interpretation needed for valuation is there exists a pricing measure under which discounted traded prices are martingales. It does not imply that actual investors have linear utility, that all physical expected returns coincide, or that the stock price uniquely identifies physical probabilities. Different beliefs can coexist with the same current traded prices and the same replication cost.

3. Every strike is legitimate 08:35

For a European call, always begin with \(H_u=(S_0u-K)^+\) and \(H_d=(S_0d-K)^+\), where \(x^+=\max(x,0)\). If \(S_0d<K<S_0u\), exercise occurs only in the up state, and the price simplifies to

\[ c_0=R^{-1}q(S_0u-K). \]

This interior-strike case separates the value of stock received from the strike payment. Both expectations are under \(\mathbb Q\). The first component is \(R^{-1}qS_0u\); the second is \(R^{-1}qK\). Neither is an unconditional promise to deliver stock or pay the strike: each is contingent on exercise.

The interior-strike restriction is not required for an option to exist or to be priced. If \(K\le S_0d\), both states are in the money, so the payoff is \(S_T-K\) and the price is \(S_0-K/R\). The replication uses one share and a borrowing of \(K/R\). The contract still has a value; on this particular state space its optionality is never used. If \(K\ge S_0u\), both payoffs are zero and the model price is zero. For the synthetic one-step market, strikes 70, 100, and 130 give prices 33.33333333, 11.90476190, and zero. A state-space assumption that rules out a payoff is economically consequential, especially when a coarse tree is used for far out-of-the-money contracts.

4. Backward induction is a trading strategy 17:31

Extend the same factors over two periods. With \(S_0=100\), terminal stock prices are 144, 96, and 64. The middle node can be reached by either up-then-down or down-then-up because multiplication commutes. A European call with strike 100 has payoffs 44, zero, and zero.

At the first up node, apply the one-step formula to the remaining two states; at the first down node, the claim is worth zero. This gives

\[ c_u=26.19047619,\quad c_d=0, \qquad c_0=15.58956916. \]

The root holdings are \(\Delta_0=0.65476190\) shares and \(b_0=-49.88662132\). Before rebalancing at date one, the portfolio is worth \(\Delta_0S_1+b_0R\), which equals \(c_u\) or \(c_d\). At the up node, replace it with \(11/12\) shares and cash \(-83.80952381\). Stock costs \((11/12)120=110\), so the new portfolio costs \(110-83.80952381=26.19047619\). At the down node both holdings are zero. Node wealth pays for the new portfolio without external funds; this budget identity defines self-financing in the discrete model.

At the up node the cash debt becomes 88 in the final period. Stock worth 132 or 88 leaves 44 or zero. On a first down move, the zero portfolio leaves zero whichever move follows. This verifies all four ordered paths, including both histories reaching the same middle stock price. The lab checks these budget and terminal-payment identities directly.

In general, for \(n\) steps and a terminal payoff \(g(S_n)\), set

\[ V_{n,j}=g(S_0u^jd^{n-j}), \qquad V_{i,j}=\frac{qV_{i+1,j+1}+(1-q)V_{i+1,j}}{R}. \]

Here \(j\) counts up moves by date \(i\). At each node, the same two-equation replication establishes the recursion. Induction proves that its root cost replicates the terminal payoff on every path. With constant parameters this is also

\[ V_0=R^{-n}\sum_{j=0}^n {n\choose j} q^j(1-q)^{n-j}g(S_0u^jd^{n-j}). \]

The binomial coefficient counts ordered paths. It is not an extra probability assumption: the conditional pricing up probability is \(q\) at each step in this specified tree. Backward induction needs \(O(n^2)\) operations and \(O(n)\) stored values. A terminal-stock payoff permits node recombination. An Asian payoff depending on the running average generally needs an additional state variable: equal stock prices alone do not imply equal future contractual obligations.

5. Reproducing the source put and its exercise decision 18:21

The lecture's two-year put uses \(S_0=50,K=52,u=1.2,d=0.8\), two annual steps, and a continuously compounded rate \(r=0.05\). Its bank factor is \(R=e^{0.05}\), rather than 1.05. Thus \(q=0.6281777409\). Terminal prices are 72, 48, 32, with put payoffs zero, 4, 20. The lab recomputes full-precision values; rounded values are shown here:

Node Stock European value Immediate put exercise Value with exercise on tree dates
First up 60 1.414753 0 1.414753
First down 40 9.463930 12 12
Root 50 4.192654 2 5.089632

At the down node, continuing has value about 9.46, while immediate exercise delivers 12. A holder with exercise rights therefore chooses 12. Backward induction for exercise-eligible dates becomes

\[ V_{i,j}=\max\left\{g(S_{i,j}), R^{-1}[qV_{i+1,j+1}+(1-q)V_{i+1,j}]\right\}. \]

At the root, continuing is worth about 5.09, exceeding the immediate exercise receipt 2. Intrinsic value at the root is 2, while the option value is about 5.09. The difference is the value of retaining the exercise opportunity rather than taking the current receipt.

The recursion is also a finite optimal-stopping argument. At the last decision date, the holder takes the larger of exercise and continuation. Conditional on that optimal subsequent policy, make the same comparison at the preceding date. Induction reaches the root. Allowing exercise on more dates cannot reduce value because every previously admissible exercise policy remains available.

A two-date tree implements exercise at its specified dates. It does not examine every instant in a year. It is best read as an approximation to an American contract, or exactly as a contract with exercise on those dates. Continuous-time American valuation requires a suitable finer model and a convergence argument. A non-dividend stock call with nonnegative rates supplies a useful contrast: at an exercise date, retaining the call has value at least \(S-K/R\), which is at least \(S-K\) when \(R\ge1\); hence immediate exercise cannot improve value. This comparison assumes no intervening dividends and the same borrowing/lending terms.

6. Uniqueness depends on spanning, not on notation

Add a third possible terminal stock value, while keeping only stock and cash traded. Let \(S_0=100,R=1.05\), and terminal stock prices 80, 100, 120. Three payoff equations cannot generally be solved with two holdings. For the strike-100 call, the first two equations would force \(\Delta=0,b=0\), contradicting the up-state payoff 20. The claim is not spanned.

Pricing probabilities must sum to one and satisfy the stock equation. Writing them \(q_1,q_2,q_3\), these constraints give

\[ q_1=a,\qquad q_2=0.75-2a,\qquad q_3=a+0.25. \]

Every \(0<a<0.375\) makes all three weights positive and prices the existing stock and bank correctly. It assigns the call price

\[ c_0=\frac{20(a+0.25)}{1.05}, \quad 4.76190476<c_0<11.90476190. \]

For each such price the positive state prices preclude arbitrage: any nonnegative terminal portfolio payoff, positive in at least one possible state, has a strictly positive weighted present value and cannot have zero cost.

The endpoint arbitrages can also be constructed directly. At the lower endpoint, buy the call, short one share, and deposit $100/R$. Initial cost is $c_0-100+100/R=0$; terminal wealth is $(20,0,0)$ in the three stock states. The missing positive pricing weight was precisely on the profitable state.

At the upper endpoint, buy half a share, borrow $40/R$, and short the call. Its initial cost is $50-40/R-c_0=0$ and its terminal wealth is $(0,10,0)$.

Both are arbitrages because all three states are physically possible. A price beyond either endpoint adds an initial receipt to the corresponding construction; depositing that receipt preserves nonnegative terminal wealth. The open interval is therefore deliberate. Extra preferences, a calibrated model, or another traded security may select a price within it; no-arbitrage alone does not. The binomial model's unique pricing measure comes from full rank of its two-state payoff matrix.

7. A diffusion limit requires shrinking moves

Fix a horizon \(T\), let \(h=T/n\), and choose \(u=e^{\sigma\sqrt h},d=e^{-\sigma\sqrt h},R=e^{rh}\), with \(\sigma>0\). For sufficiently small \(h\), strict no-arbitrage holds. Expanding the pricing weight gives

\[ q=\frac12+\frac{r-\sigma^2/2}{2\sigma}\sqrt h+O(h^{3/2}). \]

A log-price increment is \(+\sigma\sqrt h\) or \(-\sigma\sqrt h\). Its pricing mean is therefore \((r-\sigma^2/2)h+O(h^2)\), and its variance is \(\sigma^2h+O(h^2)\). Independent increments over \(n\) steps give limiting log-return mean \((r-\sigma^2/2)T\) and variance \(\sigma^2T\). Moment agreement alone does not prove a distributional limit. Here the increment sizes vanish and are uniformly bounded by \(\sigma\sqrt h\), so the triangular-array central limit theorem applies: its Lindeberg condition is eventually satisfied because no centered increment can exceed any fixed positive threshold. Thus terminal log price converges in distribution to the normal law of risk-neutral GBM.

Convergence of prices for an unbounded call needs one more step. The second moment satisfies \(\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}S_T^2=S_0^2[qu^2+(1-q)d^2]^n\), which stays bounded as \(n\) increases because the bracket is \(1+(2r+\sigma^2)h+O(h^2)\). This bound makes terminal stock prices uniformly integrable; the call is bounded by stock. Distributional convergence therefore extends to convergence of call expectations after truncating the payoff and controlling the tail by the second moment. This is a terminal European-payoff result, not a proof of continuous-path convergence or American optimal-stopping convergence. Cox–Ross–Rubinstein, section 5, especially manuscript pages 20–21 treats the lognormal limiting construction.

For \(S_0=K=100,r=0.05,\sigma=0.2,T=1\), the lab compares successively finer trees with the limiting call formula developed in the next two chapters. Convergence is a model-consistency check. It does not establish that the diffusion accurately represents market jumps or trading costs.

8. Laboratory and model boundary

Run the standalone binomial laboratory. It checks direct state matching, physical-versus-pricing expectations, backward induction against an independent terminal-sum calculation, self-financing on every two-step path, the source put, exercise-date monotonicity, strike boundaries, and the three-state price family. Synthetic parameters are separate from the source reproduction.

The tree is exact for its own state space. It does not prove that actual returns have two outcomes or that rebalancing is free. Refining a tree also requires specifying how \(u,d,R\) change with the time step; keeping fixed 20% annual moves while multiplying the number of steps changes the modeled risk. The next chapter connects a shrinking-step construction to diffusion pricing and examines why a continuous-time change of measure needs additional conditions.

  1. The put pays zero at 120 and 10 at 80. Thus \(\Delta=-10/40=-0.25\), and \(b=(1.2\cdot10)/(1.05\cdot0.4)=28.57142857\). Initial cost is \(-25+28.57142857=3.57142857\). Terminal cash is 30; the short stock costs 30 or 20 to cover, leaving zero or 10. This put replication has negative delta and positive cash.

  2. Short one share, receive 100, and deposit it at factor 1.2. Initial net cost is zero. Terminal cash is 120; covering the share costs 120 in the up state and 80 in the down state. Gains are zero and 40. The down state has positive physical probability, so this is an arbitrage. The algebra gives \(q=1\), but then the pricing measure ignores a possible profitable state. Equivalence of physical and pricing null events has failed.

  3. The first-date claim values are \(26.19047619\) and zero. Hence root delta is \(26.19047619/40=0.65476190\). Cash is \(-0.8\cdot26.19047619/(1.05\cdot0.4)=-49.88662132\).   4. Set \(R=1.05\), yielding \(q=0.625\). Terminal prices and payoffs remain unchanged. At the up node, the put value is \(0.375\cdot4/1.05=1.42857143\).   5. At \(a=0.1\), probabilities are \((0.1,0.55,0.35)\), giving \(20\cdot0.35/1.05=6.66666667\). At \(a=0.3\), they are \((0.3,0.15,0.55)\), giving \(10.47619048\).   6. Up-then-down gives prices \((100,120,96)\), average \(316/3=105.33333333\), and payoff \(16/3=5.33333333\). Down-then-up gives \((100,80,96)\), average 92, and payoff zero. Store the running sum, or another equivalent sufficient state, in addition to current stock.

"""Binomial checks; no files, network, or trading effects."""

from itertools import product
from math import comb, erf, exp, isclose, isfinite, sqrt


def close(actual, expected):
    assert isclose(actual, expected, rel_tol=1e-11, abs_tol=1e-10), (
        actual, expected
    )


def parameters(spot, up, down, growth):
    values = (spot, up, down, growth)
    if not all(isfinite(x) for x in values):
        raise ValueError("Parameters must be finite")
    if not (spot > 0 and 0 < down < growth < up):
        raise ValueError("Require positive spot and 0 < down < growth < up")
    return (growth - down) / (up - down)


def replicate(spot, up, down, growth, high, low):
    """Return cost, shares, and current cash for a one-period claim."""
    q = parameters(spot, up, down, growth)
    delta = (high - low) / (spot * (up - down))
    cash = (up * low - down * high) / (growth * (up - down))
    cost = delta * spot + cash
    close(delta * spot * up + cash * growth, high)
    close(delta * spot * down + cash * growth, low)
    close(cost, (q * high + (1 - q) * low) / growth)
    return cost, delta, cash


def tree(spot, up, down, growth, steps, payoff, exercise=False):
    """Return all layers, indexed by date then number of up moves."""
    q = parameters(spot, up, down, growth)
    if not isinstance(steps, int) or isinstance(steps, bool) or steps < 1:
        raise ValueError("steps must be a positive integer")
    layers = [None] * (steps + 1)
    layers[steps] = [
        payoff(spot * up**j * down**(steps - j))
        for j in range(steps + 1)
    ]
    for i in range(steps - 1, -1, -1):
        layer = []
        for j in range(i + 1):
            continuation = (
                q * layers[i + 1][j + 1]
                + (1 - q) * layers[i + 1][j]
            ) / growth
            intrinsic = payoff(spot * up**j * down**(i - j))
            layer.append(max(intrinsic, continuation)
                         if exercise else continuation)
        layers[i] = layer
    return layers


def terminal_sum(spot, up, down, growth, steps, payoff):
    q = parameters(spot, up, down, growth)
    return sum(
        comb(steps, j) * q**j * (1 - q)**(steps - j)
        * payoff(spot * up**j * down**(steps - j))
        for j in range(steps + 1)
    ) / growth**steps


def verify_paths(layers, spot, up, down, growth):
    """Check each rebalance costs current wealth and final wealth matches."""
    steps = len(layers) - 1
    for path in product((0, 1), repeat=steps):
        price, wealth, j = spot, layers[0][0], 0
        for i, move in enumerate(path):
            cost, delta, cash = replicate(
                price, up, down, growth,
                layers[i + 1][j + 1], layers[i + 1][j]
            )
            close(cost, wealth)
            price *= up if move else down
            wealth = delta * price + cash * growth
            j += move
            close(wealth, layers[i + 1][j])


def main():
    # Endpoint prices support explicit zero-cost nonnegative payoffs.
    stock_states = (80, 100, 120)
    call_payoffs = (0, 0, 20)
    lower = 100 - 100 / 1.05
    upper = 50 - 40 / 1.05
    assert abs(lower - 4.761904761904759) < 1e-12
    assert abs(upper - 11.904761904761905) < 1e-12
    assert tuple(c - s + 100 for s, c in zip(
        stock_states, call_payoffs)) == (20, 0, 0)
    assert tuple(.5 * s - 40 - c for s, c in zip(
        stock_states, call_payoffs)) == (0, 10, 0)
    s, u, d, growth = 100.0, 1.2, 0.8, 1.05

    def call(price):
        return max(price - 100, 0)
    q = parameters(s, u, d, growth)
    price, delta, cash = replicate(s, u, d, growth, 20, 0)
    close(q, 0.625)
    close(price, 250 / 21)
    close(delta, 0.5)
    close(cash, -800 / 21)
    physical_mean = 0.8 * 120 + 0.2 * 80
    close(physical_mean, 112)
    close(q * 120 + (1 - q) * 80, 105)
    print(f"One-step call {price:.8f}; physical stock mean {physical_mean}")

    layers = tree(s, u, d, growth, 2, call)
    verify_paths(layers, s, u, d, growth)
    close(layers[0][0], terminal_sum(s, u, d, growth, 2, call))
    print(f"Two-step call {layers[0][0]:.8f}; all four paths replicated")
    for steps in (1, 3, 12, 30):
        result = tree(s, u, d, growth, steps, call)[0][0]
        close(result, terminal_sum(s, u, d, growth, steps, call))

    def source_put(price):

        return max(52 - price, 0)
    source_growth = exp(0.05)
    european = tree(50, u, d, source_growth, 2, source_put)
    american = tree(50, u, d, source_growth, 2, source_put, True)
    verify_paths(european, 50, u, d, source_growth)

    close(american[1][1], european[1][1])
    assert american[0][0] > european[0][0] > 2
    print(f"Source q {parameters(50, u, d, source_growth):.10f}")
    print(f"Source up/down {european[1][1]:.8f}, {european[1][0]:.8f}")
    print(f"Source European {european[0][0]:.8f}")
    print(f"Source exercise-grid value {american[0][0]:.8f}")
    simple_e = tree(50, u, d, growth, 2, source_put)[0][0]
    simple_a = tree(50, u, d, growth, 2, source_put, True)[0][0]
    close(simple_e, 4.251700680272107)
    close(simple_a, 5.136054421768708)

    for strike in (70, 100, 130):
        value = replicate(s, u, d, growth, max(120 - strike, 0),
                          max(80 - strike, 0))[0]
        expected = {70: 100 - 70 / growth, 100: price, 130: 0}[strike]
        close(value, expected)

    # Expanding the same fixed exercise grid cannot reduce its value.
    continuation = european[1]
    q = parameters(50, u, d, source_growth)
    up_only = (q * max(0, continuation[1])
               + (1 - q) * continuation[0]) / source_growth
    close(up_only, european[0][0])
    assert american[0][0] >= up_only

    for a in (0.1, 0.3):
        weights = (a, 0.75 - 2 * a, a + 0.25)
        close(sum(weights), 1)
        close(sum(w * x for w, x in zip(weights, (80, 100, 120))), 105)
        assert min(weights) > 0
        value = 20 * weights[2] / growth
        assert 5 / growth < value < 12.5 / growth
        print(f"Three-state a={a}: call {value:.8f}")

    def cdf(z):

        return (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2))) / 2
    limiting_call = 100 * cdf(0.35) - 100 * exp(-0.05) * cdf(0.15)
    errors = []
    for steps in (40, 160, 640):
        step = 1 / steps
        up, down = exp(0.2 * sqrt(step)), exp(-0.2 * sqrt(step))
        value = tree(100, up, down, exp(0.05 * step), steps, call)[0][0]
        errors.append(abs(value - limiting_call))
        print(f"Diffusion-grid n={steps}: call {value:.8f}")
    assert errors[2] < errors[1] < errors[0]
    assert errors[-1] < 0.004
    close((100 + 120 + 96) / 3 - 100, 16 / 3)
    close(max((100 + 80 + 96) / 3 - 100, 0), 0)
    for args in ((100, 1.2, 0.8, 1.2), (0, 1.2, 0.8, 1.05)):
        try:
            parameters(*args)
        except ValueError:
            pass
        else:
            raise AssertionError("Invalid market was accepted")
    print("All binomial and exercise checks passed.")
